His actions potentially placed his parole and freedom at serious risk.

## 4. Interview Method

Conduct this as a **one-question-at-a-time memoir interview**.

Do not issue a questionnaire containing ten or twenty questions at once.

Begin chronologically. The first objective is to put the author physically back into Stacey’s mother’s house immediately after release and allow the memory to develop from there.

Start with:

> **Take me back to when you first came home after prison and were living at Stacey’s mum’s place. What do you remember about arriving there and what the house felt like in those first few days?**

Then follow the author’s recollection.

Prefer questions that recover physical environment, who was present, household routines, ordinary interactions, relationships between household members, narrator’s perceived position in the household, specific sequences of events, genuinely remembered dialogue, timing relative to Polytech, and consequences and decisions.

Do not continually interrupt a productive recollection to impose a predetermined question sequence. When a memory opens another relevant memory, follow it while preserving enough chronology to return to the main sequence.

## 5. Critical Narrative Distinction — Family

Do not reduce this period to:

**violent young man gets into trouble after prison.**

That framing would miss the point.

The material appears to expose something fundamental about the narrator’s existing moral code.

He regarded certain people as family and therefore believed protecting them was his responsibility.

Whether they reciprocated that sense of belonging is a separate issue.

The potentially important emotional contradiction is:

**he was prepared to put himself—and potentially his newly regained freedom—at risk for people he considered family, despite not necessarily being regarded as family himself.**

Do not state that proposition directly in memoir prose unless the author naturally articulates it.

The preferred method is to reconstruct events accurately enough that the reader recognises the contradiction independently.

## 6. Character Continuity Rule

The memoir must not portray culinary school as creating the narrator’s morality.

His underlying code predates cooking: respect, loyalty, family, keeping one’s word, protecting people, doing one’s part, not taking liberties, and expecting comparable conduct from others.

What changes over the memoir is not necessarily the **principle** but the narrator’s repertoire for expressing, enforcing and applying those principles.

This distinction has already appeared in `STARTING MONDAY`.

When younger students disrespect Glen, the narrator identifies conduct he genuinely regards as wrong. His proposed remedy—inviting one outside—is disproportionate by ordinary classroom standards but internally coherent.

Glen does not correct the principle.

He **redirects its application**.

Locked language from the chapter:

> He didn’t make a big deal of it. Didn’t raise his voice.
>
> Just redirected me.

This continuity is important when reconstructing the household violence. Do not retrospectively turn the narrator into either a hero or a villain to make the material easier to interpret.

Recover his actual reasoning at the time.

## 8. Writing Standard for Reconstructed Material

The target is not a retrospective summary.

The reader should feel as though the narrator has been placed back inside the memory and is replaying it.

Use physical continuity, ordinary connective actions, remembered environmental details, social behaviour, genuinely supported internal reactions, genuinely remembered dialogue, and selective short sentences where the moment earns them.

Avoid excessive fragmentary prose, artificial cinematic description, invented sensory detail, manufactured dialogue, retrospective psychoanalysis, moralising, sanitising uncomfortable facts, making violence heroic, making violence a generic redemption device, and explaining every social dynamic to the reader.

The recent `STARTING MONDAY` revision demonstrated that sentence sequencing materially affects reading quality.

Short lines are most effective when the scene itself creates the pressure for them. Ordinary connective movement should remain where it makes the memory feel lived rather than assembled.

## 9. Authenticity / Non-Fiction Constraint

This is memoir.

**Do not invent facts to improve the writing.**

Uncomfortable factual material should not be removed merely because it is uncomfortable.

This includes, where historically true and relevant, drug use, criminal-world behaviour, violence, parole restrictions, hostile internal reactions, family dysfunction, and contradictions in the narrator’s behaviour.

The author has explicitly established that uncomfortable reality is often precisely what makes the memoir relatable.

If a fact is uncertain, ask. If the author cannot remember it, preserve that uncertainty where appropriate rather than filling the gap.
