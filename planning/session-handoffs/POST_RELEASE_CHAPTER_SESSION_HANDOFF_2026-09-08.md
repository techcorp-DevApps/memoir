## 4. Established prose/voice rules from this session

These are now governing constraints for this chapter:

- Default cadence is flowing, conversational prose.
- Avoid repeated short, staged, jolted sentences except where the scene genuinely earns them.
- Do not use constant one-line paragraphs to manufacture emphasis.
- Avoid repeated social shorthand such as `our world`, `where we came from`, `people like us`, and similar constructions.

- Different scenes have different textures; do not flatten all discomfort into one word.
- Factual accuracy overrides thematic neatness.
- Do not invent dialogue, emotional reactions, gestures, or connective events.
- Author-restored earlier wording outranks a newer assistant rewrite.
- When assembling approved blocks, reproduce them; do not "improve" them in transit.

I kept my head down. Read when I could.

Eventually I got released early for good behaviour.

It was a cracker of a day when I walked out.

Cold, obviously. Invercargill wasn’t suddenly going to stop being Invercargill just because I’d got out of prison.

Stace was there waiting for me.

I didn’t have much. Whatever personal property I’d had when I was taken into custody, the bits and pieces that had made their way into my cell, and a cheque for two hundred and eighty dollars.

**Steps to Freedom.**

That was genuinely what it was called.

If you’d been locked up for six months or more, the New Zealand Government apparently decided two hundred and eighty bucks was enough to launch you successfully back into society.

Which was nice of them.

We walked into town and cashed it.

Then we went home.

Or, more accurately, we went to Lynn’s.

Lynn was Stace’s mum. I’d met her before, but only a couple of times, and now I was moving into her house directly from prison, so there was probably room for things to be slightly awkward.

It was a small state house down a long shared driveway in a quiet cul-de-sac. Nothing particularly remarkable about it except that Lynn kept the place immaculate. Stace and I had the bedroom off the kitchen.

I put my stuff down.

That was moving day taken care of.

I knew Melissa well already. We got on easily and she’d become more like family than just Stace’s sister. Nakita I barely knew; not surprisingly, given how young she was, we hadn’t spent much time around each other.

Lynn welcomed me in properly, although there was some awkwardness underneath it. I couldn’t tell whether she actually wanted me living there or whether she wanted Stace there badly enough that I came as part of the package.

Probably a bit of both.

Stace and Melissa hadn’t lived with their mother since they were infants. If Stace wasn’t staying there, there was a reasonable chance Melissa wouldn’t either.

So there I was.

Fresh out of prison, living with Stace in her mother’s spare bedroom while everyone worked out exactly what this new arrangement was supposed to look like.

I had people to see, plenty of whom didn’t know I was out yet.

I used part of the $280 to buy half an ounce of buds.

Just weed.

Given some of the other shit that turns up later, that clarification is probably worth making.

Stace and I kept enough for ourselves and I moved the rest on. By the time it was gone, I had the original money back and about another hundy on top.

Not a bad return on the Government’s reintegration package.

After that we went around to an old mate’s place. It was one of those houses where somebody was always coming or going, the door was rarely shut for long, and news travelled considerably faster than anybody involved.

If you wanted people to know you were back, you didn’t need to make phone calls.

You just went there.

By that evening, word was getting around.

We headed back to Lynn’s because she was cooking roast lamb shoulder.

And Lynn’s roast lamb was fucking sensational.

It was one of those meals you looked forward to when you knew it was on. Proper roast lamb, vegetables, the whole lot, done the way she did it.

The fact that I had spent six months eating prison food certainly didn’t hurt.

During the rounds that afternoon someone had given me a bottle of homemade moonshine as a welcome-home present, so that came out as well.

Lynn barely drank.

The rest of us were somewhat more committed to the exercise.

There was still a bit of awkwardness around the table at first. Lynn and I barely knew each other, and within a few hours of getting out I was already living under her roof with Stace.

There probably isn’t a completely natural small-talk transition for that.

Fortunately, awkwardness and homemade moonshine have a fairly predictable relationship.

By the end of the night, most of it had disappeared.

Between the roast, the moonshine and being back beside Stace, things had started to feel almost normal.

The only complication was that Lynn’s house was quiet.

Really quiet.

The sort of place where everybody could hear everybody else doing practically anything.

We were young, I’d just got out, and Stace had spent six months waiting for me. Apparently discretion wasn’t going to be part of the reunion.

Our bedroom was off the kitchen. Lynn was only a few metres away.

Stace did not seem remotely troubled by either fact.

That became fairly obvious on the first night.

The moonshine might have taken care of the awkwardness for the evening.

Morning brought some of it back.

Nobody mentioned the night before, but there was enough of it hanging around to suggest the moonshine had probably been helping more than any of us realised.

It didn’t last particularly long because everyone had their own shit to do, and I had to report to parole.

At first that meant going in every morning. My parole officer was an older Māori bloke and a fucken good cunt. He wasn’t interested in making the process any more complicated than it needed to be. If I turned up on time and wasn’t obviously drunk or stoned, there wasn’t much reason for either of us to spend all morning staring at each other. Some days I’d sign in, stick my head through the door so he knew I was there, and be back outside again a few minutes later.

That suited me fine. I’d get parole out of the way, then carry on with whatever I had lined up for the day. I was still making money the way I knew how, but I wasn’t interested in adding burglaries, stolen cars, fights or any of the other shit that gave police extra reasons to come looking.

Living at Lynn’s settled into its own rhythm as well. I was conscious that I’d arrived with almost nothing and moved straight into somebody else’s house, so once I had a bit of money behind me I gave Lynn a few hundred dollars.

I don’t remember her asking me for board. I just didn’t like the idea of eating her food, using her power and sleeping under her roof while contributing nothing.

Around the same time I started putting things on lay-by at Farmers. Decent cookware, towels, blankets, crockery, all the boring household stuff that becomes fairly important once you realise you don’t actually own any of it.

Stace didn’t know.

That was intentional.

We’d lived together before I went inside, and Lynn’s was somewhere we’d landed rather than somewhere I expected us to stay forever. I didn’t know when we’d manage to get another place, but I wanted to start putting things aside for when we did.

So for a while that became the routine. Parole in the morning, whatever I needed to do during the day, back to Lynn’s afterwards, and every so often another payment toward a collection of household shit for a home we hadn’t found yet.

I came home one morning through the back door and found Shane sitting in the kitchen with Lynn.

That was unexpected.

More importantly, judging by Lynn’s face, I wasn’t supposed to know he was there.

Shane was Nakita’s father and Lynn’s on-again, off-again partner. He and I already had history, most of it stemming from an earlier incident involving Lynn that had given nineteen-year-old me what I considered a perfectly legitimate reason to fight him.

Protective instinct was part of it.

So was the fact I liked fighting more than I probably should have.

Either way, we weren’t mates.

Lynn looked caught.

Shane looked determined not to.

For a second the three of us just stood there in the kitchen pretending there was nothing remotely awkward about any of it.

“Morning.”

Something like that.

I walked through to the bedroom.

Stace was still asleep, so I told her when she woke up. Melissa found out later.

As far as they knew, Lynn had agreed Shane wouldn’t be around.

Apparently that arrangement had become negotiable.

For the first little while his visits were kept quiet. Then the effort slowly disappeared. He came around more often, everybody knew, and within a couple of weeks there wasn’t much point pretending anymore.

Shane was just back.

To his credit, Shane made a genuine effort to keep the peace with me. Offered me a sesh. Tried making small talk.

I wasn’t a fan of small talk even with people I liked.

But it was Lynn’s house. As long as he left her and the girls alone, I was prepared to leave him alone too.
