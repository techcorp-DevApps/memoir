# I CAN COOK BETTER THAN YOU

I've been doing this long enough to know what I'm worth.

Long enough that my hands don't feel heat the way yours do. Long enough that my
body keeps score in ways I stopped noticing years ago. Long enough that I've
stopped counting the kitchens, the covers, the chefs who broke me down and the ones
I broke down in return.

So when someone tells me they can cook, I listen.

And then I wait for the punchline.

Because it's always coming. It's always some version of: "I made a risotto once and
it turned out great." Or: "My partner does this amazing Thai curry." Or my personal
favourite: "My Mum's the best cook I know."

I get it. You think you can do what I do.

But here's the thing—you can't.

There are countless differences between my cooking environment and yours. My
kitchen stove top does not have a "Save As" button, okay? If I fuck up in the
kitchen, that's it. There's no undo, there's no: "Oh well, the guests will
understand."

Guests will NEVER understand. Nor do they appreciate that I can cook a steak to
medium rare, every time. Because when my guest is paying fifty-eight dollars for
that steak, do you really think they're going to be satisfied when it's not right?
A simple: "Oh that's okay love, medium rare, give or take a few degrees." They may
as well be saying: "Here, while you're at it take my wallet and the keys to my
Mercedes E350... And I'm an understanding, laid-back guy—of course you may fuck my
wife."

You just sit in your cushy little household kitchen with your nine-hundred-dollar
KitchenAid that you have only mustered how to whisk in, and casually stress over
trying to pull the garlic bread up at the same time as Donna Hay's Pasta a la
Primavera without everything going stodgy and cold for your five other guests who
will probably still accept you at least as a casual acquaintance even if it is only
for a Christmas party invite—sadly, my reviewers are not the same.

I'll have seven pans going, three of them bipolar and temperamental sauces, two
fish in the oven, a salad to prep, all while watching the kitchen hand with one eye
who keeps wiping the plates clean with a tea towel without actually washing
them—whilst not forgetting that table seven is half gluten-free even though it's
not on the docket, and tables thirty-four, seventeen, and three are coming up on
mains all at once because front of house are cunts.

No worries, mate.

But it's even different for us chefs at home. I guarantee you, when I make a meal
it's all: "Thanks handsome man, please pass the potatoes." But you bet your arse if
my girl is cooking for me, I better act like she's single-handedly built the Great
Wall of China, blindfolded, with nothing more than a toothpick and some intuition.

---

But as far as your Mum being the best chef you know—shush, just stop it. Your Mum
might be the best cook in the world, for sure. Hell, my mother makes the best bbq
chicken I have ever tasted. And my Dad? Well... he wasn't much of a cook, but his
curried sausages. I can guarantee you, it can't be beaten. So I do believe you when
you say your half-Lebanese mother makes the best... I don't know, falafel.
Whatever—my point is, they make it for you. They make it for your brothers and
sisters and family friends.

They don't make it every single night, for fifty-five or one hundred and five
covers, plated perfectly, cooked perfectly, over and over, repeatedly and
consistently—and fast.

My Dad can't do that. Do you know WHY he can't do that?

Because he's a fucking Cabinet Maker, not a chef.

*"No one is born a great cook, one learns by doing." — Julia Child*

I like that quote. I like to think that the techniques I have repeated day in and
out for however many years mean something. I like to think that what we do is kind
of like porn. You can watch it, be fascinated by it, it will entice your intrigue
and tickle your curiosity—but try it at home, it won't look or sound nearly the
same or as satisfying, and the majority of you are sensible enough to realise that
it's probably not wise to try and act it out.

You can like cooking all you want. It doesn't mean it will like you back.

And it's the little things that make us different. For example:

- I can chop without looking.
- I don't follow a recipe. I laugh at recipes. I look at them, study them, then
  laugh at them and think: "Well fuck, that's not gonna happen—I'm doing it this
  way."
- I can pick up a piece of confit duck with my bare hands. It doesn't bother me one
  bit; I lost all feeling in them years ago anyway. The sensation in my index
  finger is left with the skin that is now attached to the grill of my first job.
  There it shall remain.
- We don't have Band-Aids; we have masking tape.
- Where my palms once were, I now have scars and calluses.
- I don't have an eight o'clock reservation for two at a delightful little wine bar
  with my significant other—I have a second sitting, a headache, and I'm running
  out of leeks.

Do you see me walking up to a neurosurgeon suggesting that: "Hey, I played
Operation as a kid. I can do this shit too."

No. I'm not that fucking stupid.

So please. Stop trying to patronise me and my fellow industry comrades by telling
me you can do what I do. You can't. And when you try, you just look like an idiot.

When you try—you do things like go on MasterChef.

And mate, I've got the popcorn ready.

---

But here's the thing about confidence like this—it doesn't come free.

You don't walk into your first kitchen with opinions. You walk in with nothing.
Less than nothing. You walk in as the problem everyone has to work around until you
figure out how to stop being one.

And if you're lucky—really lucky—you find someone brutal enough to turn you into
something useful.

But it's not just the brutal ones who shape you.

It's the ones standing next to you when everything's on fire.
