# THE PEOPLE WHO LAUGH WHILE YOU'RE DROWNING

There's a very specific kind of love that exists in real kitchens.

Not brunch joints with latte art and matching aprons.

I mean proper rooms. Serious ones. The kind where the food almost never goes
wrong—and if it does, everybody remembers the date and time like a funeral.

From the outside, it probably looks like bullying.

From the inside, it's the only reason any of us still have a pulse.

It always hits me on those nights when the rail is full and nobody's breathing.

You know the ones.

Walk-in already stripped. Prep list still half-checked. Private function in the
back. Full book in the front because management likes saying "yes" to every booking
and "no" to reality.

By six o'clock the docket rail is doing that slow, dangerous lean. Grill is
snarling at the printer. Pastry's already tight because desserts changed yesterday
and nobody told her until the mise was done.

I'm in my corner trying to keep my station immaculate and my head clear, working at
that speed where every movement is deliberate and nothing can be sloppy, because in
rooms like this "near enough" means "do it again".

It's when you're running that close to the line that you feel it—that awful
tightness in your chest that whispers:

*If you slip, you'll take everyone with you.*

Then it starts.

The chorus.

**"BEHIND".**

**"HOT".**

**"YES CHEF".**

**"IN THE OPEN YOUR FUCKEN EYES SECTION".**

To anyone else, it sounds like aggression.

To us, it sounds like breathing.

---

There was one Saturday I still feel in my bones.

Fully booked from six till late. Tasting menu on. Couple of VIPs the owner wouldn't
shut up about.

My station was veg and garnish—which, in a good kitchen, is not "just veg". It's
pace and precision and timing and discipline. Every plate walks past you. You're
the hinge on every main.

Calls start stacking:

> *"away on tasting mains, table 15 mains, all together."*
>
> *"how long entrees table seven."*
>
> *"three minutes on scallops table twenty"*
> *"yes chef"*

We're in the groove—not loose, just tight in the right way. Setup's sharp, plates
are landing clean, everything hitting that "of course it's perfect" level you live
for.

Then one docket drops with a mod that changes everything on my side: no alliums, no
butter, separate pan for one component. Totally doable, but it means a different
pathway in my head.

I hear it, I repeat it, I mark it... and somewhere between "yes, Chef" and my hands
moving, my brain tries to process two dockets at once.

For half a heartbeat, I reach for the wrong pan.

I don't sauce it. I don't plate it.

I catch myself a fraction of a second before anything irreversible happens and
shift to the clean pan. The guest will never know how close that got.

But inside my head?

That half-second wobble feels like I've crashed the entire service.

Chef clocks it immediately.

Of course he does.

> *"Garnish how long. mains—two beef, one duck, one veg. You've got purée, beans,
> potatoes, and the mod on ten. Call your times."*

"Four on beef and duck, five on veg, six on the coeliac."

> *"three minute on mains—two beef, one duck."*

My world shrinks around me.

Grill adjusts his own timing by a minute without saying anything, just calls:

> *"Beef is plus one, Chef."*

Like it was his decision. Buying me time.

Fish slides an extra pan onto my free burner with the component I nearly
forgot—no lecture, just a tap on the bench.

Pastry, miles from the line, somehow feels the shift in volume and sends a ramekin
of something set and sugary to my station. She drops a spoon next to it and
mutters:

> *"Eat or I'll murder you."*

Which in kitchen language translates to: Your blood sugar is tanking and I'm not
carrying your dead body out of here.

Nothing about it looks soft.

Nobody says, "Are you okay?"

Chef still calls me a muppet when I reach for the salt instead of the tongs.

But the whole kitchen tightens around me just enough that there is zero chance of
an actual fuck-up hitting a plate.

The guests get what they always get:

Exact. Precision. Perfection.

No hint that, for about thirty seconds, one of the cogs in the machine felt like it
was about to shake itself loose.

---

After service, we're out in the alley. Backs against the wall. Staffies sweating in
our hands. Everyone running on fumes and adrenaline.

Everything hurts.

Feet, shoulders, fingers, the bit of your spine you only discover exists after
twelve hours on the pass.

Someone brings up a ticket we turned around in a way that should've been
impossible. Someone else reminds Chef of the time he called a re-fire on a whole
table because he decided, unilaterally, that the plates had sat in the pass twenty
seconds too long.

Then, because we're arseholes, they circle back to me.

> *"Remember when apprentice almost baptised the allergy plate in jus tonight?"*

Grill says it with the grin already loaded.

They all laugh—its not to be cruel.

It's recognition.

They've all had that half-step slip, that almost-mistake nobody outside the room
ever knew about.

Grill nudges my boot with his.

> *"Didn't happen though, did it? That's the point."*

Chef doesn't join in the pile-on. He just looks over, takes a drag of his
cigarette:

> *"alright lets go."*

People hear how we speak to each other and think we hate each other.

They hear the swearing, the sarcasm, the way "cunt" gets used as both insult and
endearment, and they assume it's toxic.

They don't see the other half.

The senior chef who quietly re-does your mise after service, not because it was
unusable, but because he wants tomorrow to start clean for you.

The grill cook who takes the bollocking that should've been yours because you were
the one who misheard the call, and he decides it stops with him.

The dishie you'd sell your soul for because they keep your world moving, and if
anyone from front of house talks down to them, suddenly the whole brigade is
standing there, very polite and very, very dangerous.

We will call each other every name under the sun from six till ten.

But if someone outside the line takes a shot at one of ours?

We remember we're a pack.

You might be "an idiot" at 7:05 when you forget a garnish, but at 7:06, if a waiter
says something sideways, you're:

*"My idiot, actually."*

---

That's the thing about this life.

You don't earn your place once. You earn it every service. Every shift. Every time
you show up and prove you're still worth keeping.
