# THE EARLY DAYS

## Draft v0.1 — December 2025

---

# CONCURRENT

The charges kept stacking.

Two years. Five years. Six years.

I stopped doing the math after the third count. Grand theft auto. Cultivation with intent to supply. Burglary. Then the smaller ones—common assault, possession, obtaining by deception, proceeds of crime, breach of bail conditions.

Eighteen charges in total.

The works.

The main charges alone carried a maximum of eight years. I was twenty-three.

I stood when they read the final count. Saluted my brother in the gallery. My best mate. My girlfriend. Tried to make it look casual, like I'd be back in a week.

The bailiff's hand on my elbow wasn't rough. Just firm.

The door to the side room closed behind me with a sound like a walk-in freezer sealing shut.

---

Fifteen minutes.

That's how long I sat in that room waiting for my lawyer. Felt like fifteen hours.

Walls beige. Table bolted to the floor. Water stain on the ceiling shaped like New Zealand, which felt like some kind of cosmic joke I was too stressed to appreciate.

The door opened.

My lawyer walked in looking oddly pleased with herself. Manila folder tucked under her arm. The kind of expression you have when you've just talked your way out of a parking ticket.

"What the fuck are you so happy about?"

She blinked. Set down the folder.

"You got concurrent sentencing. That's excellent."

"Concurrent?" I said. "I got six years. How is that excellent?"

She stared at me. I stared at her.

"Do you know what concurrent means?"

"Yeah," I said.

But even as the word left my mouth, I realised I didn't. Not really. I'd thought it meant one after another. Stacked up. *Consecutive*. That was the word I'd been thinking of.

"You'll serve them at the same time," she explained. Speaking to me like I was a child who'd just asked why the sky was blue. "The cultivation charge is one year. You'll serve that while you're serving the others. You're not getting six years total. You're getting one year. Maybe less with good behaviour."

I sat back in the bolted-down chair.

One year.

Not six.

The relief didn't come. Not then. Just a hollow ringing in my ears, like I'd been standing too close to a speaker at a gig.

I signed the papers. Didn't read them.

What was the point?

---

# HIGH RISK OF REOFFENDING

Three months in, I applied for home detention.

The prison board laughed at me.

Not literally—they were far too professional for that—but the woman reading my file had this smile. Tight. Thin. Like she was trying not to laugh in front of me.

"You've breached every single bail condition ever imposed on you," she said, running her finger down the page like she was reading a shopping list. "Every. Single. One."

I didn't argue. It was true.

"We're assigning you a high-risk-of-reoffending status. Don't bother reapplying."

---

I hated jail.

Not for the reasons you'd expect.

Not the violence. Not the food. Not the strip searches or the fact that you shit in a room with another man three feet away. I could handle all that. I was young. Strong. Not cocky, but confident in my ability to hold my own.

People left me alone.

No.

I hated jail because of the boredom.

And the bullshit.

Every single person in there had a story, and ninety percent of those stories were lies. Badly told lies. The kind of yarn-spinning that might work on someone who'd never left their suburb, but if you had even a handful of brain cells, you could see right through it.

I kept my head down. Read when I could. Worked in the kitchen—ironic, in hindsight—peeling potatoes, washing dishes, staying out of everyone's way.

Got released early. Good behaviour.

But the conditions were crippling.

---

At first, it was daily reporting. Show up in person. Every single day. Pop my head in the door, wave, prove I wasn't drunk or high, leave.

My first parole officer was this old Māori bloke. Absolute good cunt. Didn't hassle you as long as you were on time and sober. Some days I didn't even have to go all the way to his office. Just stuck my head in, waved, signed off.

Then he retired.

And everything went to shit.

---

# THE WOMAN WHO CHOSE COOKING

Her name was Sarah. Or maybe Sharon. I honestly can't remember, which tells you how much I wanted to forget those meetings.

She was young. Late twenties. Fresh out of whatever degree you need to become a parole officer. By the book in a way that wasn't cruel—just relentless.

*This is the policy and I will follow it to the letter.*

Somehow worse than cruelty. You can't argue with policy.

The first time I met her, I was ten minutes late.

"You're late."

"Yeah, bus was—"

"I don't need to hear it. Sit down."

Her office was small. Beige. Stacked with files and a dying pot plant on the windowsill. Photo of a golden retriever on her desk.

Everything about the room said: *I care very much about rules and also dogs.*

She flipped open my file.

"High risk of reoffending."

"Yep."

"You've breached every bail condition."

"Yep."

"You've been arrested how many times?"

"Lost count."

She looked up at me. Not unkindly. Not warmly either. Just assessing.

"You need a job."

"I'm looking."

"You need structure."

"Yeah."

"You need to be somewhere every day where people are watching you."

She picked up the phone. Dialled. I sat there while she spoke to someone on the other end.

> "Hi, yes, I'm calling about your courses. What's starting in the next week?"

Long pause. Scribbling on a notepad.

> "Commercial cookery? Level two? When does that start?"

Another pause.

> "Perfect. I'm enrolling someone. I'll send the paperwork today."

She hung up. Looked at me.

"You're starting a cooking course on Monday."

"What?"

"Commercial cookery. Level two. Polytech. Every day, nine to five. Miss a day, I'll breach you, and you'll go back to finish your sentence. Understand?"

I stared at her.

"I don't want to be a fucking cook."

"I don't care what you want." Not meanly. Just factually. "You need structure, supervision, and a skillset. This is what's available. You start Monday. Nine a.m."

"What if I want to do something else?"

"Like what?"

I didn't have an answer.

"That's what I thought."

# FIRST DAY

I was up at 0630.

Prison schedule. The body remembers.

I shaved my head—did that most mornings—trimmed my goatee, put on the clothes my mum had bought me the day before. Black steel-cap boots, polished, 12-up laced. Dress pants—not business pants, not slacks, somewhere in between. Collared polo shirt. All black.

Mum and I didn't have the best relationship back then. Still don't, really. But she'd taken me shopping. Paid for everything. Didn't say much. Just handed over her card when I picked out what I needed.

A gesture of intent, I suppose.

I rode my BMX to polytech. Full panic mode by the time I got there. Not because I was scared of the course—I wasn't scared of anything like that. But I wasn't a civilian. Not by any definition. Hadn't been since I left school at thirteen. I had nothing in common with anyone in that building.

Nothing.

I parked my bike against the wall outside the classroom. Didn't lock it. Didn't need to. Who the fuck would be crazy enough to steal my bike?

That was the mindset.

Rolled a cigarette. Had a smoke. Definitely nervous about the environment—completely out of my comfort zone. But confident in myself. I always was.

I could carry my own. No problem.

---

I finished the smoke. Walked in.

The classroom was theory only. About twenty students—different ages, different backgrounds. School leavers through to people in their mid-thirties. Everyone talking amongst themselves.

Then I walked in.

Everyone stopped.

Looked at me.

I was definitely out of place in that room.

Then they went back to talking. A little more quietly.

The tutor was mid-forties. His name was Glen. Wool cardigan. Slacks. Leather business shoes. The kind of attire I would've laughed at in another life.

But I had respect for others by default. Whether you kept that respect was your choice—and it was decided quickly based on how you conducted yourself.

Strange thing about life in criminal circles. There's still a moral code of sorts.

I walked straight up to Glen and shook his hand. Introduced myself.

It was something I'd always done—even with close friends when entering a room. Shake hands. I don't know why. It just felt right. Asserted my confidence, I suppose. Despite being very introverted, I had a very dominating personality when I needed to.

Glen nodded. Shook my hand. Didn't seem nervous.

"Take a seat wherever you like."

I found a spot at the back. On my own.

---

The morning passed.

Theory. Introduction to the course. What we'd be covering. The format—theory, theory, practical, theory test, practical test. Repeat.

Some of the younger ones kept talking during the session.

I lost my shit.

"Show some fucken respect when someone's trying to teach us something. Other people actually want to learn."

The room went quiet.

One of the lads had a little giggle.

Which sparked me up more.

"Something funny, mate? Want to step outside with me?"

The kid went bright red. Shrank back in his seat.

That's when Glen intervened. Carefully. Not backing down, but not escalating either.

> "I appreciate the sentiment. But that's not how we handle things in a classroom environment."

I understood.

I respected it.

Didn't mean I liked it.

But it was no different to jail, really. There's a time and a place, and this wasn't it. And at the end of the day, I was trying to achieve the same goal as Glen—people paying attention, showing respect. Undermining him would've been contradictory.

So I nodded.

Sat back down.

The kid stayed inside during the first break. Didn't come out with everyone else.

Fair enough.

# THE CLOWN SUIT

The practical sessions started a few days later.

I hated the uniform.

Little hat. All whites. Those blue-and-white checked pants—looked like something a clown would wear. I felt like a fucking idiot.

But the knives.

The knives were something else.

Brand new Wüsthof set. Fucking choice. The weight of them. The shine. The way they sat in the roll like they were waiting for something.

I took them home that night to show Stacey.

Laid them out on the kitchen table like they were jewellery. Showed her each one. The chef's knife. The paring knife. The boning knife. All of it.

She smiled. Didn't say much. Just let me show her.

That was the first thing I'd taken home in a long time that didn't need to be hidden or explained away.

---

The first practical unit was salads, sandwiches, dressings, and roasting.

We made focaccia from scratch. Made a mayo. Made a salad. Roasted a little ham. Then assembled a sandwich.

I didn't know what the fuck I was doing with the bread.

I knew how to cook—I'd been out of home for a long time, always fairly poor, but I could feed myself. Roasts. Bolognese. Even the odd batch of cookies. Basic survival stuff.

But focaccia? Dough? Proving? Technique?

That was different.

I was in my own zone, though. The other students disappeared. It was just me and the bench and the task in front of me. I wasn't going to ask for help. I'd figure it out. That's what I'd always done.

I only just finished in time.

But I did it.

Took a big ham salad sandwich home for Stacey that night.

Fucking choice.

---

# EVERY SUNDAY

Stacey.

We'd been together for several years by then.

She'd come to see me every Sunday in jail. Both times. Rain, hail, snow—that girl walked to the prison every week for an hour behind glass with me.

Came to my parole hearing.

Came to court.

She was on board with anything that meant I wouldn't end up back inside.

She knew my life up until then. All of it. And it didn't seem to phase her much—despite being brought up by her grandparents with her three sisters. Very strict household. The opposite of everything I was.

She'd bet on me before there was anything to bet on.

I told her she was nuts when she said she wanted to move in together. I had nothing. Less than nothing.

She did it anyway.

---

# THE GRIND

I found it challenging.

The whole way through.

Not just the first week or the first month—the entire course. Every unit. Every practical. Every exam. It didn't come naturally to me the way some things did. I had to grind for it.

But I did.

By the end of the second year, I'd made it into the top percentile. To get invited into the final year, you had to score in the top bracket. I did.

During the final year, I was also invited to sit the City and Guilds exam.

Passed with distinction.

For the first time, I started to think: *Maybe this is actually going somewhere.*

---

# GRANT'S KITCHEN

I finished the course and started looking for work.

Given my location—bottom of the South Island—there wasn't much. So I started looking in Auckland. I'd lived there before. Knew the city.

Found a job listing on Seek. Applied just like any normal person would have to.

*Just like any normal person.*

That phrase meant something. Applying through proper channels. Waiting for a callback. Going through an interview. The kind of thing people do every day without thinking about it. For me, it was new territory.

They were looking for a junior level chef. With my qualifications and the City and Guilds distinction, I fit the requirements well.

No need to disclose anything prior.

Which worked out just fine.

---

The restaurant was small. Maybe fifty seats. Usually just a single sitting.

There was Grant—the head chef—me, and a kitchen hand. On Fridays and Saturdays we'd have an additional hand to help with cold sides.

Grant was an older guy. Relaxed. Pretty sure he was a high-functioning alcoholic—something I later learned to be fairly normal in the industry. He held it together well enough, and the food wasn't all that technical. Basic stuff, done well.

The pace was the thing.

In culinary school, you focus on one dish until it's done. In an actual service, you're running three tickets at once and they all need to land at the same time.

That took getting used to.

---

About six months in, Grant pulled me aside.

> "I've got a feeling the owners are going to sell. It's on the cards."

He said it calmly. Like he was telling me the weather.

> "I'm going to take some leave before that happens. I want to get you ready to run service while I'm gone."

He brought in a temp—someone he knew well, someone who'd be a good help during that stretch—and that's what happened.

I ran that kitchen.

Even small. Even basic food. Even with help.

I ran it.

And it went fine. Better than fine. I'd thought I could do it before Grant told me I could. Him confirming it—him trusting me enough to leave—that meant something.

When he came back, he confirmed the sale was going through.

> "Start looking now. Don't wait until they make the announcement. You don't want to be caught in the cold."

So I started looking.

Found a listing for a vineyard restaurant in New Zealand.

Applied.

Got the call.
