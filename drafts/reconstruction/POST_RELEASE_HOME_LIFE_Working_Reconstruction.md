# Post-Release Home Life — Working Reconstruction

> **Working status:** This document is pre-draft reconstruction material only. It is not an approved chapter, not promoted manuscript prose, and not canon. It exists to preserve chronology, scene detail, voice fragments, contradictions, factual anchors, and potentially useful material for later block-by-block assessment and manuscript development.

Release morning was a cracker of a day.

Cold, obviously. Invercargill wasn't suddenly going to stop being Invercargill just because I'd got out of prison.

But clear.

Stace met me at the gates.

I didn't have much with me. Personal effects from my cell, whatever property I'd had on me when I was taken into custody at court. That was about it.

On the way out they gave me a cheque for two hundred and eighty dollars.

Steps to Freedom.

That was what they called it.

If you'd been incarcerated for six months or more, the New Zealand Government apparently decided two hundred and eighty bucks was enough to launch you successfully back into society.

Good luck.

We walked into town, cashed the cheque, and headed back to Lynn's.

────────

Lynn lived in a state house down a long shared driveway in a quiet cul-de-sac.

Small place. Three bedrooms. Single garage. Weatherboard.

Nothing flash.

But immaculate.

Lynn was an exceptionally tidy person. Everything had its place and the house always felt looked after.

You walked through the front door straight into the sitting room. Two bedrooms came off that, one either side. Across the lounge was the kitchen and dining area. Our bedroom was off there to the right.

Laundry, toilet and bathroom were to the left, with the back door leading out toward a small grassed yard.

Nakita and Melissa shared one of the bedrooms off the lounge. Lynn had the other.

Stace and I had the room out the back near the kitchen.

I dumped what little I owned in there.

That was me moved in.

The house was always incredibly quiet.

Painfully quiet sometimes.

The kind of quiet where privacy was more an optimistic concept than an actual feature of the building.

I discovered that fairly comprehensively on the first night when Stace apparently forgot there were other human beings living ten metres away.

────────

I wasn't a complete stranger to Lynn.

I'd met her a couple of times before, but only briefly.

Melissa I knew well. We got on easily and she was already much more like a little sister to me than just Stace's younger sister.

Nakita I barely knew.

Understandably. She was only little.

Stace's youngest full sister was still living back in Tuatapere, where Stace and I had been living when we first met.

Lynn welcomed me into the house.

Properly welcomed me.

But underneath that I could feel a bit of discomfort. Awkwardness maybe.

I couldn't tell if she actually wanted me there.

Fair enough, really.

She barely knew me and now I'd just come out of prison and moved into a bedroom with her daughter.

There was another layer to it too.

This was the first time Stace and Melissa had lived with their mother since they were infants.

And realistically, if I wasn't staying there, Stace probably wasn't staying there.

If Stace wasn't there, Melissa probably wasn't either.

There were a lot of things sitting underneath that arrangement that nobody needed to say out loud.

────────

We didn't hang around the house much that first afternoon.

I'd met plenty of people before jail and several more while I was inside. Some had already been released. Others still had time to go.

Two hundred and eighty dollars wasn't much.

But it was enough.

I bought half an ounce of buds.

Nothing dramatic. Just weed.

Given some of the other shit that turns up throughout this story, that clarification is probably worth making.

It gave me and Stace something for a sesh, left enough to work with, and if everything moved the way it should I could make the original money back and clear roughly another hundred dollars.

Not a bad return on the Government's reintegration package.

We went around to an old mate's place afterwards.

It was one of those houses everybody ended up at.

Not a halfway house. Nothing organised about it.

Just a place where the door was generally open, people were always coming through, and if you wanted half the town to know something there were worse places to spend an afternoon.

I'd been gone.

Now I wasn't.

Word got around.

────────

We went home that evening because Lynn was cooking roast lamb shoulder.

She did a fucking good roast.

Proper spread. Lamb, vegetables, everything.

It was me, Stace, Lynn, Melissa and Nakita around the house.

Nakita was young enough that she disappeared off to bed reasonably early.

Someone had given me a bottle of homemade moonshine during the rounds that afternoon as a welcome-home present, so that came out with dinner.

Lynn barely drank.

At least I almost never saw her drink.

The rest of us were somewhat more committed to the exercise.

Dinner was awkward at first.

Not badly.

Just that awareness that I was now living in the house of a woman I'd barely met, sleeping with her daughter in the room off the kitchen, having arrived there directly from prison.

There probably isn't a completely natural small-talk transition for that.

Fortunately, awkwardness and homemade moonshine have a fairly predictable relationship.

The bottle went down.

The awkwardness went with it.

And mostly I just remember being glad to be out.

Sitting at a table.

Eating roast lamb.

Normal shit.

────────

I adapted pretty quickly to Lynn's place.

That was something I'd always been good at.

You work out the rules of where you are, respect the place, don't become a burden.

During the week Nakita had school. I was awake early anyway. Jail hadn't exactly trained me to sleep until midday.

So I'd get up and make use of the day.

Morning runs.

Pick up money.

Reload.

Move things around.

Report to the parole office when I was supposed to.

Then head home.

I wasn't doing burglaries.

Wasn't stealing cars.

Wasn't bouncing between every possible way of getting myself arrested.

If prison had taught me anything useful about crime, it was this:

Pick your crime.

Stick with it.

People doing five different kinds of shit at the same time were the ones who seemed to constantly get themselves caught for one of them.

Whether that qualifies as rehabilitation is probably debatable.

────────

Once I had a bit of money behind me, I went into Farmers.

Big New Zealand department store. Clothes, beds, towels, cookware, everything in between.

I started putting things on lay-by.

Good cookware.

Towels.

Blankets.

All the boring little household things you don't think much about until you have none of them.

I didn't tell Stace.

I wanted it to be a surprise.

We'd already lived together before I got locked up and Lynn's was never supposed to become our permanent home.

I had fuck all.

And I didn't want that to mean Stace had fuck all too.

Getting our own place would take some time, but I could at least start putting the pieces together.

I also gave Lynn a few hundred dollars.

Realistically it was probably more than her rent cost in those days.

But that wasn't the point.

I wasn't sitting in someone else's house, eating their food and using their power while calling myself a grown man.

I didn't want to feel like a bludger.

────────

Things at Lynn's were generally good when Shane wasn't around.

Shane was Nakita's father.

He and Lynn had an on-again, off-again relationship and I already knew him before I moved into the house.

His family lived around the small rural town where Stace and I had been living and Shane and I had crossed paths before.

There had also been an earlier incident where I heard he'd assaulted Lynn.

At that stage I hadn't even properly met Lynn.

But she was still Stace's mum.

That had been sufficient justification for me.

Looking back, protecting somebody was part of the reasoning.

So was the fact that I was young, angry, liked fighting more than I should have, and had found myself a morally acceptable excuse to do it.

Both things can be true.

Shane himself was a loud, cocky prick.

About ten years older than me, capable of handling himself, which at that age only made the prospect more interesting.

I didn't like him.

More importantly, I didn't trust him.

But once I was living at Lynn's, I also understood something pretty simple.

It was Lynn's house.

She was an adult.

Her relationship was her business.

Until it wasn't.

────────

At first Shane coming around again was kept secret.

Stace and Melissa had made it clear to Lynn that they didn't think the relationship was healthy and didn't want him around. Lynn had agreed.

Shane also knew how to manipulate her fairly easily.

The first time we realised he'd been staying there again was almost accidental.

Nakita was at school.

Melissa was out.

Stace was still asleep.

I'd been out doing my morning routine—money, contacts, parole—and came back through the rear door.

Lynn and Shane were sitting in the kitchen.

They obviously thought everybody was out.

Lynn looked like a deer in headlights.

Shane did the opposite.

That awkward kind of confidence where somebody acts like there's absolutely nothing strange happening because admitting there is would require acknowledging the obvious.

We'd already had our history.

He remembered it.

I remembered it.

Neither of us needed to discuss it.

We exchanged brief hellos.

I walked through to our bedroom and shut the door.

That was it.

Stace got told as soon as she woke up.

Melissa later that day.

For the next couple of weeks the pretence slowly disappeared until eventually it became more or less:

Fuck it. Everybody knows anyway.

Shane started being around openly.

To his credit, he made a genuine attempt to keep the peace with me.

Offered me a sesh.

Tried making small talk.

I wasn't a fan of small talk even with people I liked.

But again:

Lynn's house.

Lynn's relationship.

Unless he did something to Lynn or the girls, I really couldn't have given a fuck.

────────

By the time New Year was approaching, things were moving financially.

One of the boys I'd met inside had been released and made contact.

He had a significant LSD connection in Dunedin.

That was about three and a half hours away and I didn't even have a car, but he got me up there, put me up for the weekend, and we talked business.

I wasn't in a position to buy at the scale he wanted.

He apparently wasn't interested in waiting.

So he fronted me a thousand tabs.

Come back in a month.

Pay for those.

Next time bring cash for half.

Suddenly I was sitting in Southland with a supply of LSD nobody else seemed to have.

Great position if you can sell it.

Less useful if you can't.

It moved more slowly than some of the other things I had going on, so the stockpile grew while everything else kept turning over.

I didn't know it then, but that LSD would eventually matter.

It would become part of how I financially survived the later years of culinary school.

Eventually it would fund the move to Auckland when I finished studying and got my first proper job.

None of that existed in my head yet.

There wasn't some master plan.

At that point it was just another thing I had found a way to turn into money.

────────

About two weeks before New Year I asked Lynn if I could put on a small get-together at the house.

Nothing huge.

Us.

My little brother Steven—CJ to most people by then.

My best mate Si, who was being released.

Shane included.

Bit of a welcome home.

Bit of New Year.

Lynn said yes.

So I organised it.

Steaks.

Sausages.

Charcoal for the Weber.

Another supply of moonshine.

Chairs out in the shed.

Stereo set up.

Barbecue ready.

────────

Si was staying with his mum about a forty-five-minute walk away and arrived early that morning.

He stood out against almost everyone I knew.

He was Māori, for one. At the time virtually every other close friend I had was European.

Tall.

Lean.

Steel-cap work boots worn down from years of walking everywhere.

Never shorts.

Jeans or cargos, equally worn.

Some kind of metal-band T-shirt.

And the same oversized green bomber jacket I swear he wore through rain, hail, summer, winter and probably natural disaster.

That jacket deserves its own story.

So does the way we met.

Another time.

Steven arrived around mid-morning with his best mate Sam, who also happened to be Stace and Melissa's cousin—Lynn's brother's son.

And the day got underway.

The older boys were already experienced drinkers.

Years of drinking moonshine close enough to neat because mixers cost money and apparently we had standards about fiscal responsibility.

We let the younger boys drink lightly.

But they had to eat first.

That rule was enforced.

The strange juxtaposition was that nobody seemed particularly concerned about them smoking buds with us.

Apparently lunch was the moral line.

────────

Nakita was around but mostly stayed inside.

Lynn came in and out of the shed through the early part of the day.

Stace and Melissa were around.

Everything was fine.

Then sometime after lunch, Shane arrived.

With his father.

His father was already fairly intoxicated.

Nobody had invited him.

Nobody particularly wanted him there.

There had been strong allegations circulating over the years about him having inappropriate involvement with underage girls.

No charges had been laid.

I didn't know whether the allegations were true.

What I did know was that Si and I were both aware of them, neither of us liked the man, and his presence immediately changed the atmosphere.

Lynn looked shocked to see him.

She tried to hide it.

He was Shane's father, after all.

A man in his early fifties at the time.

I was less interested in hiding my opinion.

I made it clear he wasn't welcome.

That got explained away.

He wasn't staying.

Just dropping Shane off.

Quick drink.

Then he'd be gone.

Fine.

────────

He didn't leave.

By about eight that evening he was heavily intoxicated.

Still light outside—it doesn't get properly dark down that end of New Zealand until somewhere around ten-fifteen or ten-forty-five during summer.

Shane and Lynn were moving in and out of the shed.

At some point Shane's father made a pass at Melissa.

She didn't make a scene.

Just did that uncomfortable little social movement people make when they don't know how to respond without making the situation worse.

Ignored him.

Shifted her chair further away.

I saw it immediately.

Not just what he'd done.

Her body language.

"All good, Melissa?"

Loud enough that everybody heard.

She didn't really answer.

She got up instead and moved across to stand with the boys and talk with Stace.

The next time Shane came into the shed I gave him the warning.

Get your old man moving before he does something stupid and gets hurt.

Shane dismissed it.

Said he was harmless.

Then leaned over and said something quietly to his father.

I never heard what.

Maybe it didn't require a response.

Maybe the old man didn't think he owed one.

Maybe he was simply too drunk.

Shane said he'd be leaving shortly.

Just finishing his drink.

────────

Maybe half an hour passed.

The old man finished his drink and stood up.

Good.

Done.

He walked out toward the garden and took a piss.

Nothing unusual about that. Half the boys had probably done the same thing by then.

Then he came back.

Opened his cooler.

Pulled out another drink.

And with all the fucking cheek in the world sat down beside Melissa.

Then put his hand on her knee.

Everything changed instantly.

"Oi!"

It wasn't a question.

It was the kind of shout designed to stop behaviour through sheer interruption.

Everybody's attention moved.

His beer disappeared out of his hand.

Bottle hit the concrete.

Smashed.

I grabbed him by the scruff of his shirt and physically threw him out of the shed.

"Fuck off while you've still got the chance to walk."

He was so drunk he could barely organise himself.

He'd landed on his hands and knees on the concrete and cut them up.

The smashed glass and shouting brought Shane back.

And now he had a problem.

Whatever his father had done, it was still his father.

And in the world we came from, that counted.

He started advancing toward me.

That was a mistake.

From basically every possible angle.

────────

Steven and Sam saw what was coming.

They tried to defuse it.

They knew me.

They knew that when it came to somebody I considered family, volatile was probably the generous description.

Add a good quantity of moonshine and things needed to be brought under control quickly.

They weren't.

I told the younger boys to sit down.

Sam did.

Steven tried one more time.

Not with me.

With Shane.

He told him what his father had done and said just get him out of there.

For a moment, it worked.

Shane went up the driveway after his father.

There was a break.

His father got into his vehicle and left.

It could have ended there.

────────

Then Shane came back.

His father had scraped his hands and knees when I threw him out.

Shane was angry about it.

"He's just a harmless old man."

That did not improve the situation.

Not after what I'd just watched him do to Melissa.

At that age, I wasn't someone who believed in waiting politely for another person to throw the first punch.

If I decided violence had become necessary, I acted.

Shane and I went toe to toe.

The neighbour came outside.

Lynn came out.

Si was trying to get between things before either of us did serious damage.

Generally, fights in our world were one-on-one.

Whatever else we were, there were rules around that.

You didn't jump someone because you had more bodies.

You handled your own problem.

Then Shane threw a wild one.

Caught Si.

Wrong bloke.

Wrong moment.

Now Si was pissed.

And Shane suddenly had both of us.

────────

The girls were screaming.

Proper chaos now.

Lynn had called the police.

She was yelling that fact at everybody, presumably hoping the words themselves would stop the fight.

Stace understood the implication immediately.

Police meant arrests.

An arrest for me didn't just mean an arrest.

I was on parole.

Lynn hadn't called them because she wanted somebody dragged away in cuffs.

She wanted an authority capable of stopping what was happening.

A formal, enforceable end to the madness.

What she didn't seem to understand in that moment was that once you call police to an active violent disturbance, you don't get to decide what they do when they arrive.

Shane made a run up the driveway trying to get away from Si and me.

And with timing that would have been funny in almost any other circumstance, the police car was coming down the driveway at exactly the same time.

So what they saw was Shane running directly toward them from what they would have treated as a domestic-assault incident.

They immediately assumed he was the aggressor.

Which, depending on which thirty seconds of the evening you were assessing, wasn't entirely wrong.

But it also wasn't the whole story.

And for me, standing back at the house on parole with a violent altercation behind me and police now climbing out of a car—

that was where the night became something else.

────────

It was New Year's Eve.

That worked in my favour.

Police were busy.

Shane being known to them for domestic violence connected to that address worked in my favour too.

The first officers had barely begun dealing with him before another unit arrived.

By then Shane was resisting and giving the first cops a hard time.

That more or less decided where their attention was going.

Lynn had disappeared back inside.

I made myself scarce.

Not dramatically.

It barely seemed necessary.

The police had arrived, found somebody they already knew at an address they already associated with domestic incidents, and he was now actively making their job harder.

From their perspective, problem located.

Problem removed.

They took Shane and left.

New Year's Eve.

Plenty more calls waiting.

────────

We didn't see Lynn again for the rest of the night.

The rest of us carried on.

Literally.

Music back on.

Drinks.

Whatever was left on the barbecue.

The festivities continued almost as if nothing had happened.

That probably sounds insane if your normal social gatherings don't occasionally involve fistfights and police cars.

For us?

Not especially remarkable.

Everybody there had seen enough outrageous shit over the years that the interruption barely killed the party.

Shane's arrest mattered.

Just not immediately.

It was the beginning of the end of the arrangement at Lynn's rather than the event that ended it.

────────

Lynn knew her phone call had ultimately resulted in Shane being arrested.

Even if Shane's father had started the chain of events.

Even if Shane himself had escalated things once police arrived.

That sat badly with her.

From the perspective of most of us, there was another issue underneath it.

Melissa was her daughter.

Someone should have been protecting her before I ever needed to intervene.

The feeling among us was that Lynn hadn't done that.

Not just that night.

More generally.

Lynn would probably have seen that differently.

So would Shane.

But from where we stood, the person who should have drawn the line hadn't.

I had.

And everything after that became somebody else's problem.

────────

Lynn became distant.

We barely saw her around for a while.

Shane disappeared too.

Stace and Melissa didn't seem particularly surprised by Lynn withdrawing.

Neither did I.

From what I had seen—and from how the girls understood their relationship with her—Lynn had never been consistently present for Stace and Melissa in the same way she had been for Nakita.

She could put the time in when it suited.

When it didn't, she had no particular problem making that obvious.

After about a week, things cooled down again.

Or at least settled.

Nobody was under any illusion everything had been fixed.

It just stopped being openly hostile.

────────

About three weeks later Shane started appearing again.

Around the same time, my parole officer changed.

And the atmosphere in the house became progressively more difficult.

Stace and Melissa were pissed off with Lynn.

Arguments became common.

Shane avoided me like the plague.

Probably sensible.

The problem was that avoiding each other didn't make the living arrangement any less fucked.

We had nowhere else to go yet.

I was already trying to build toward moving out, but I hadn't got everything in place.

So we stayed.

────────

Then the petty shit started.

The shed would suddenly be locked at night.

It had never been locked before.

My bike was in there.

Which meant I couldn't get to it.

Which meant walking.

Everywhere.

Including across town to report to parole.

Annoying.

Deliberate or not, I couldn't prove it.

Then my dog disappeared.

Again.

────────

The first time she got loose, I never worked out how.

She was chained and getting off that chain should not have been possible.

But we found her reasonably quickly at a service station nearby.

The second time she disappeared, another household in the cul-de-sac had taken her.

That incident connects to another set of events involving Melissa that belongs elsewhere in the reconstruction.

Then she disappeared a third time.

This time she never came back.

Melissa told me she had overheard Shane saying he was going to kill her.

My dog.

Not Melissa.

We could never prove Shane had anything to do with her disappearance.

I never found out what actually happened.

That uncertainty needs to stay exactly where it is.

But I trusted Melissa.

Especially about something like that.

She had no reason to lie to me.

And the dog was gone.

For me, that was enough.

Time to leave.

────────

Me and Stace didn't need much.

Possessions weren't what made us happy.

The actual problem was finding somewhere to live.

It would be the first place I'd ever rented properly on my own.

Most of my life I'd rented a room in somebody else's place.

The closest I'd come to anything different was living with Si.

That house had been left to him and his siblings after his father died, and Si was the only one actually living there.

A rental application in my own name was new territory.

That meant I needed Ma.

────────

Me and Ma had a turbulent history.

Very turbulent.

But when the cards were genuinely on the table, she had a habit of coming through.

She also knew Shane and his family.

Properly.

They lived across the road from her in Tui—Tuatapere—so I didn't have to spend much time explaining what sort of environment I was trying to get out of.

What I needed was for her to drive us around looking at places.

And, I assumed, go guarantor.

That second part took some convincing.

────────

Ma had never had great financial stability after Dad died.

But she was incredibly resourceful.

Very good with money.

Excellent credit.

So asking her to financially attach herself to something I was doing was not a small request.

I had to convince her the situation at Lynn's was bad enough to warrant it.

And that I wasn't doing anything dodgy anymore.

I'm reasonably certain when I told her that, she knew I was full of shit.

Not completely full of shit.

Just selectively.

I wasn't suddenly legitimate.

But I also wasn't being reckless in the same way I had been before.

There was structure.

I was reporting to parole.

I had a fixed routine.

I was still turning up to cookery school.

That last one became useful.

────────

I may have slightly overinflated my enthusiasm for culinary college when talking to Ma.

Slightly.

In reality, I still couldn't have cared less about becoming a chef.

I wasn't waking up inspired by food.

I wasn't discovering my calling.

I was still there because Sarah had put me there.

But if continuing to turn up solved two problems at once?

I was on board.

It kept the parole officer happy and off my back.

And it gave Ma something tangible to look at and think:

Maybe he actually is making an effort.

That was useful.

Attendance.

Parole compliance.

A routine.

Something on paper.

Whether my private motivation matched the story being told was somewhat less important.

────────

Our rental budget was low.

Very fucking low.

But this was Southland more than twenty years ago.

Around a hundred and eighty dollars a week was our ceiling, and that could still get you something.

We weren't fussy.

Didn't care if the place was fashionable.

Didn't care about a view.

We wanted:

Clean.

Dry.

Not in a shit part of town.

Cheap.

Turns out once you ask for all four at the same time, things get slightly harder.

We spent about three days looking.

Most of what we saw was fucked.

Then we found the flats.

────────

Three single-storey units running down a driveway.

Communal laundry area up at the top.

An older couple lived in the first flat.

The middle one was available too.

I didn't want it.

Being stuck between two other households didn't appeal to me.

Privacy.

Security.

Too much movement around you.

The last flat, right down the end of the drive, made more sense.

Stace was with me when we looked.

It was tiny.

Barely enough room to swing a cat in.

But it was clean.

On a good street.

A hundred and fifty a week.

Under budget.

At the end of the driveway.

And close to Polytech.

After three days of looking at absolute shit, we barely needed to discuss it.

This'll do.

────────

You walked through the front door straight into the kitchen.

The lounge was open-plan immediately beside it.

Two bedrooms came off the main living area.

One double.

One single.

Free carpet.

Luxury.

The application process was almost laughably easy compared with what I had expected.

It was Southland.

Different era.

There wasn't some forensic examination of my rental history.

Nobody seemed particularly interested in the fact that I'd recently been released.

In the end Ma didn't even need to sign as guarantor.

The real barrier was money.

Two weeks' rent as bond.

Two weeks in advance.

And roughly another two weeks' worth to actually move in.

A little under a thousand dollars all up.

Not much money in the grand scheme of things.

For us?

A fucking fortune.

Ma fronted it.

Keys handed over.

Just like that.

Easier than expected.

────────

The funny part was that now we had a house and nothing to put in it.

Actually nothing.

A mattress.

A television.

Our clothes.

And even those were still at Lynn's.

All that shit I'd been quietly putting on lay-by at Farmers?

Very useful eventually.

Absolutely useless while it still belonged to Farmers.

We moved out that day anyway.

No waiting.

No gradual transition.

Keys in hand.

Back to Lynn's.

Grab our shit.

Gone.

────────

The most difficult negotiation of the entire housing process turned out not to be convincing Ma to pay the move-in costs.

It was convincing her to let me tie the mattress to the roof of her car.

She had an hour's drive back out into the country afterwards.

She was not thrilled with the plan.

Somehow I won.

So the mattress went on the roof.

TV in the car.

Clothes.

That was basically our entire household.

────────

Then Ma looked at what we actually had.

Mattress.

TV.

Clothes.

That's it.

"What are you going to cook with?"

Good question.

I told her I had everything sitting on lay-by at Farmers.

Cookware.

Towels.

Crockery.

Blankets.

The lot.

I didn't ask her to clear it.

I asked whether maybe she could just pay off the towels.

I'd work the rest out.

She paid the whole fucking thing.

All of it.

Suddenly the stupid little preparation I'd started weeks earlier mattered.

Cookware.

Crockery.

Towels.

Blankets.

Household shit.

Not furniture.

Not luxury.

But enough to function.

Ma came through.

Big time.

────────

She didn't hang around long once everything was unloaded.

She still had the drive back out into the country.

So eventually it was just me and Stace.

Our first place.

Empty except for a mattress, a television, some clothes, and a pile of brand-new household gear we suddenly owned outright.

We were young.

So we did exactly what you would probably expect a young couple to do the moment Ma's car disappeared and the door closed.

There isn't really a PG version of that first night.

Nor does there particularly need to be.

The place was christened.

Thoroughly.

────────

For me, home had always been where my head landed.

By that point I'd moved more times than a lot of people probably do in their entire lives.

A building didn't mean security to me.

People did.

Stace was different.

But she was happy.

She was almost always happy if she was with me.

I knew that then.

It wasn't something I only realised years later.

I just couldn't understand it.
