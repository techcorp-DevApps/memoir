# Vocabulary Context Profile

This profile directs word choice in *The Long Road To Nowhere*. Use it alongside the project's voice-context-profile to produce prose with greater precision, sensory substance, intellectual range and contextual force.

The intended result is the author's own writing operating with a more exact and resourceful vocabulary. The vocabulary must belong to the person telling the story, the world being described and the particular moment in the narrative.

Anthony Bourdain's *Kitchen Confidential* supplies the craft reference. Its useful example is the ease with which professional terminology, ordinary speech, physical description, cultured vocabulary, vulgarity and discerning judgement coexist. None of those registers is sufficient on its own. Their value comes from choosing the register that can express the observation most accurately.

**Governing instruction:** Improve the precision and expressive range of the language while preserving factual meaning, the author's voice, the established rhythm and the purpose of the passage. A more impressive word that weakens any of those things is a worse choice.

## 1. Authority and the relationship between profiles

### 1.1 What each authority controls

| Authority | Controls | Consequence for this profile |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Current author instruction and explicit corrections | Intended meaning, authorised scope, preferred wording and exceptions | Follow the author's direction. Do not turn this profile into an obstacle to an expressly requested change. |
| Established factual sources and author-confirmed recollection | Events, chronology, people, actions, material conditions and what was known | Vocabulary may articulate those facts; it may not manufacture or enlarge them. |
| Latest author-approved or expressly protected wording | Settled language within its stated scope | Preserve it unless the author authorises revising it. A general quality check alone is not that authorisation. |
| Voice-context-profile, interpreted with current author rulings | Narrative identity, viewpoint, tone, humour, rhythm, beat, emotional stance and structural behaviour | This companion must fit that voice. It cannot replace it with Bourdain's persona or sentence patterns. |
| This vocabulary-context-profile | Lexical precision, range, register, connotation, sensory specificity and repetition control | Apply within the boundaries above. |
| Reference book and examples in this document | Evidence of craft and demonstrations of editorial choices | Neither supplies facts or ready-made prose for the memoir. |

If two higher authorities appear to conflict, preserve the relevant wording and identify the actual conflict. Do not quietly correct a fact by changing protected text, or preserve a known factual error merely to pass a wording check. Continue work on unaffected material; seek clarification only when that conflict prevents the requested revision.

### 1.2 Voice and vocabulary are connected, but their responsibilities differ

The voice profile determines who is speaking and how their thought moves. This profile determines which words best carry that thought. A lexical change can still alter voice: replacing a blunt verb with a ceremonious one, adding a judgement, or lengthening the last clause can change the entire beat.

Therefore, every proposed vocabulary improvement must survive a voice check. Do not treat vocabulary as a cosmetic layer that can be applied after the narrator has already spoken.

The available companion inspected for this document is `context/chef-writer-context-profile.md`, internal version **2.3.0**, including its author ruling dated **13 September 2026** on repetition, scene economy and vividness. It performs the author-voice function referred to in this request as the voice-context-profile. No separate file with the exact name `voice-context-profile.md` was resolved in the inspected context folder. Do not rename either file on that basis.

The inspected profile contains examples of fragments and staccato emphasis. Those examples do not require a vocabulary pass to introduce fragments or break up flowing paragraphs. Current author directions and the passage's settled cadence determine their use. This document does not prescribe sentence lengths, paragraph lengths or a Bourdain-derived rhythm.

### 1.3 What lexical improvement does not grant

Do not use this profile to change chapter order, expand a scene, invent dialogue, alter the narrator's motives, reposition a ChefTip, or rewrite an entry or exit bridge beyond the scope requested. If a real improvement requires a broader change, identify that dependency rather than making it invisibly.

Preserving good existing language is an active editorial decision. A passage can pass this profile without a single changed word.

## 2. The lexical standard

The desired vocabulary is **concrete, discriminating, technically literate, conversationally credible and capable of range**.

It must be able to name a kitchen process without vagueness, distinguish one texture from another, describe a person's behaviour without flattening them into a label, and express a complicated judgement without sounding like an essay imposed on the memoir.

Apply these requirements:

1. **Name the actual thing.** Prefer the known cut, implement, part of the room, task or physical state over a generic substitute when the distinction matters.
2. **Choose the action before decorating it.** Look first at the noun and verb when a sentence feels weak. Another adjective is rarely the first repair.
3. **Make description observable.** Let words identify shape, resistance, movement, temperature, smell, sound or condition that the evidence supports.
4. **Use the full available register.** An informal narrator can use an exact, sophisticated word naturally. Do not confuse conversational writing with a restricted vocabulary.
5. **Keep judgement proportionate.** A forceful adjective must mean what the author intends, at the strength the evidence permits.
6. **Preserve ordinary language when it carries the moment.** `Good`, `bad`, `said`, `went`, `looked` and `delicious` can all be the right words.
7. **Spend language where the reader needs it.** Expand perception at consequential moments. Keep connecting actions clear and economical.
8. **Stop when the work is done.** Once a sentence has delivered its information or impact, do not attach an explanation of the effect it was meant to produce.

The test is information and effect gained, not syllables added. A technically exact word may compress an explanation; a plain phrase may carry more authority than the technical term.

## 4. The six working registers

Draw from these registers as needed. Do not attempt to include all six in every paragraph.

| Register | Principal function | Typical resources | Main failure to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Material | Make a thing physically legible | Skin, bone, fat, rim, handle, grain, film, residue; tacky, brittle, scorched | Description that sounds attractive but identifies no real property |
| Procedural | Show how work happens | Skim, strain, reduce, scrape, portion, plate, rest, drain | A technically stronger-sounding verb that describes the wrong action |
| Occupational and social | Locate responsibility and belonging | Apprentice, Chef, section, pass, service, docket, supplier | Imported slang or hierarchy that did not belong to that workplace |
| Evaluative and reflective | Express distinctions in judgement | Exacting, perfunctory, incongruous, disproportionate, fastidious | An inflated verdict, hidden motive or academic voice |
| Colloquial and profane | Preserve directness, intimacy, irritation or comic force | Author-established idiom, contractions, blunt phrasing, purposeful swearing | Performing a borrowed personality or escalating abuse for colour |
| Domestic and intimate | Give ordinary life its own vocabulary | Rent, keys, blanket, kettle, kitchen table, silence; specific remembered actions | Forcing the language of a professional kitchen onto every part of a life |

Movement between registers should follow the thought. Technical description may need a plain judgement at its end. A blunt observation may need one exact reflective word to distinguish embarrassment from something more complicated. Neither pattern is compulsory.

## 5. A decision procedure for every proposed change

Before changing a word or phrase, answer these questions in order:

1. **What is this passage doing?** Establishing place, describing technique, revealing character, carrying dialogue, acknowledging embarrassment, moving time forward, or delivering an already prepared payoff?
2. **What is actually established?** Separate confirmed particulars from inference and from details that merely seem plausible.
3. **Whose language is it?** The younger self experiencing the event, the older narrator describing it, a remembered speaker, or an editorial explanation outside the manuscript?
4. **What is weak?** Identify the precise problem: vague referent, inaccurate action, bland praise, repeated idea, wrong register, strained sound, or unnecessary explanation.
5. **What is the smallest useful intervention?** Retain, substitute, recast, cut, or flag for a factual answer. Do not default to expansion.
6. **What does the candidate imply?** Check degree, cause, intention, time, technical meaning, social judgement and emotional temperature.
7. **Does it belong in the sentence?** Read the entire paragraph aloud or simulate its spoken movement. Preserve contractions, emphasis and the landing point.
8. **Has the reader already received this?** Check the local passage, the chapter and relevant neighbouring chapters for the word, image and underlying explanation.

If the candidate contributes no identifiable gain, retain the original. If the gain depends on an unconfirmed detail, do not insert the detail. Use the established wording or request the specific missing fact when it matters to the task.

## 6. Nouns: locate the reader accurately

### 6.1 Prefer the relevant level of specificity

Move from a general noun to a precise one only as far as the evidence and scene require.

| General wording | More exact possibilities | Condition of use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meat | Lamb shoulder, calves' liver, pork belly, a known cut | The cut or animal is established and affects the picture or action. |
| Sauce | Jus, reduction, emulsion, gravy, the sauce's actual name | Use the preparation's correct identity. These terms are not interchangeable. |
| Pot | Stockpot, saucepan, sauté pan | The vessel is known and the distinction matters. |
| Knife | Chef's knife, boning knife, blade, tip, heel | Name the implement or working part relevant to the action. Do not invent a brand. |
| Kitchen area | Pass, section, prep bench, coolroom, wash-up area | Use the workplace's actual arrangement and vocabulary. |
| Mess | Grease, peelings, flour, spilled stock, scraps | Identify the material if it is known. `Mess` remains useful as a judgement or shorthand. |
| A place to live | Flat, unit, bedroom, tenancy, keys | Match the actual housing and the narrator's local usage. |

Do not burden a scene with model numbers, species, regional appellations, suppliers or menu names just because they would make it more specific. Unnecessary precision can read as research pasted into memory.

### 6.2 Preserve stable referents

Once established, a stockpot can remain a stockpot. Do not cycle through `cauldron`, `vessel`, `kettle` and `vat` to avoid repeating it. That introduces size, mood or equipment distinctions the scene may not support.

Use pronouns when the referent remains unmistakable. Repeat the noun when clarity requires it. Technical noun repetition is different from repeating a judgement or re-explaining an idea.

### 6.3 Names carry relationships

Keep author-established names, forms of address and capitalisation. `Chef`, a person's name, a nickname and `the head chef` can indicate different relationships. Do not vary them purely for lexical variety. Do not replace a person's chosen or established name with a descriptor that reduces them to an age, appearance or role.

## 7. Verbs: make force and process exact

### 7.1 Choose verbs for what happened

| Function | Candidate verbs | Distinction to preserve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Remove material | Skim, strain, drain, scrape, trim | Each identifies a different operation. Choose the actual one. |
| Apply or manage heat | Simmer, boil, sear, roast, sweat, scorch | These specify different conditions or results. Greater intensity is not automatically more vivid. |
| Change concentration or condition | Reduce, render, emulsify, split, set | Use only when the process described actually warrants the term. |
| Separate or shape | Slice, dice, carve, portion, joint, score | Distinguish cutting for size, shape, service or access. |
| Move a substance | Pour, ladle, spoon, smear, spread, fold | The material, implement and motion must agree. |
| Handle something | Lift, carry, drag, lever, haul, wrestle | Stronger verbs imply weight, resistance or struggle. Do not add those implications without support. |
| Attend to work | Watch, glance, check, inspect, taste | These imply different duration and purpose. A glance is not an inspection. |
| Respond to someone | Say, ask, tell, call, mutter, snap | Delivery and emotional force must be remembered or otherwise established. |

A quiet verb can communicate expertise. `Checked`, `adjusted` and `tasted` may be better than `attacked`, `hammered` and `wrestled`. Competence often consists of doing the necessary thing without theatrical force.

### 7.2 Do not put motives inside verbs by accident

`Said` does not mean `admitted`. `Looked` does not mean `leered`. `Asked` does not mean `pleaded`. `Left` does not mean `stormed out`. `Corrected` does not mean `humiliated`.

These changes can alter culpability, intention, power and emotional meaning. They require a basis in the source, not merely a preference for colourful prose.

### 7.3 Keep movement credible

Do not make every pan slam, every printer scream or every cook bark. Equipment and people need not perform the mood of the sentence. Use literal movement first; figurative force earns its place only if it clarifies the perception and belongs to the author's voice.

## 8. Material and sensory vocabulary

These are selection tools, not a bank of interchangeable adjectives. Choose the property that is actually being described. Do not add a property to make use of an appealing word.

### 8.1 Texture, structure and behaviour

| Word or distinction | What it can identify | Misuse to prevent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gelatinous | A jelly-like character or gelatin-rich texture in the relevant context | Do not weaken an established gelatinous state with `almost`; do not use it as a generic synonym for rich. |
| Viscous | Resistance to flow | It does not identify the cause of thickness or prove gelatin content. |
| Unctuous | A rich, often oily or fatty mouthfeel; also an insincerely ingratiating manner in human description | It is not a technical substitute for gelatinous. Check which sense the sentence activates. |
| Silky | Perceived smoothness and low resistance | It does not mean every desirable sauce. Establish a sensory reason for choosing it. |
| Coating / clinging | How a liquid adheres to a surface or food | State the relevant behaviour instead of multiplying nearby synonyms for it. |
| Tacky / sticky | Degrees and kinds of adhesion in context | Neither automatically means spoiled, unpleasant or gelatinous. |
| Wobbly / yielding | Movement or response to pressure | Use where observed. They do not establish flavour. |
| Tender / soft | Ease of eating or cutting versus a broader lack of firmness | Do not assume they are equivalent for every ingredient. |
| Friable / crumbly | A structure that readily breaks apart | Use the more specialised word only if it adds precision and sounds natural in the surrounding prose. |
| Brittle / crisp / crunchy | Different ways something breaks or resists the bite | Let the actual food and observation determine the term. |
| Fibrous / stringy | Visible or felt strands | May be desirable or undesirable. Do not attach a verdict automatically. |
| Leathery / rubbery | Different forms of resistance or toughness | Do not flatten both into the vague label `badly cooked` if the distinction matters. |
| Floury / grainy / gritty | Distinct particle-like sensations or structures | These are not synonyms and do not identify the same fault. |
| Greasy / fatty | A perceived surface or eating quality versus fat as a material component | Fat is not itself a criticism. Keep composition and judgement distinct. |
| Watery / thin | Dilution-like character versus low body or thickness in context | Neither alone proves the cause or quality of the preparation. |

### 8.2 Taste and smell

| Field | Candidate vocabulary | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salt and marine character | Salty, briny, saline | Use `salty` for saltiness; use the others only when the broader sensation or register warrants them. |
| Acidity and drying sensation | Sour, tart, sharp, astringent | Astringency describes a drying or puckering sensation, not simply sourness. |
| Bitterness and burning | Bitter, scorched, acrid, burnt | Separate taste, smell and the state or cause being claimed. |
| Aroma and persistence | Pungent, smoky, musty, stale, sour | `Pungent` indicates strength or penetration, not necessarily unpleasantness. |
| Sweetness | Sweet, cloying, syrupy | `Cloying` introduces excessive or oppressive sweetness; `syrupy` can also describe consistency. |
| Richness and savouriness | Rich, savoury, meaty, fatty | Identify what the reader needs. Avoid treating these as an automatic cluster. |
| Specific association | Nutty, grassy, metallic, earthy | Use only for a remembered or source-supported association. Do not invent tasting notes. |

`Rancid`, `putrid` and similar terms make strong claims about condition. They are not energetic replacements for `unpleasant`. If the source establishes only a smell the narrator disliked, do not diagnose spoilage through vocabulary.

### 8.3 Appearance, temperature, sound and residue

| Dimension | Useful possibilities | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Surface and light | Sheen, gloss, film, flecks; dull, translucent, opaque | Identify the material and the relevant appearance. Gloss and flavour are separate observations. |
| Heat and atmosphere | Warm, hot, scalding, humid, stifling | Do not escalate temperature or bodily distress to intensify a scene. |
| Contact and movement | Clatter, scrape, hiss, rattle, thud | Give the sound a source. Select the sound that matters to the action. |
| What remains | Residue, sediment, scraps, crust, smear, stain | Distinguish loose matter, deposited matter and a mark. Use the material's name when possible. |
| Condition | Worn, chipped, dented, stained, polished, spotless | Keep age, damage, cleanliness and finish distinct. |

Avoid automatic colour decoration. `Golden`, `ruby`, `emerald` and `jewel-like` do not make food writing vivid by themselves. Actual browning, translucency, staining or surface texture often contributes more.

## 9. Culinary precision and the limits of description

Use culinary terminology with enough precision that an experienced cook would recognise the process. Do not turn a vivid sentence into an incorrect technical explanation.

Particular safeguards:

- Stock, jus, gravy and sauce are not a stylistic rotation for one liquid.
- Reduction describes a process of concentration through loss of liquid; a finished texture alone does not establish every step used to obtain it.
- Gelatin, fat, starch and an emulsion are not interchangeable explanations for body, richness or cling.
- `Browning`, `caramelising`, `searing`, `scorching` and `burning` must follow the actual preparation and claim. Do not apply a more specialised term merely because it sounds authoritative.
- `Silky`, `glossy` and `delicious` describe different qualities. One does not establish the others.
- A real stock-making fault should remain the actual fault. Do not escalate a lapse into ruined stock, contamination or service failure without evidence.

When technical certainty is missing, keep to what is established or check an appropriate technical source if the task requires the explanation. *Kitchen Confidential* is the literary reference for this profile; it is not a current technical authority for every preparation or workplace practice.

### 9.1 Explain a process only as far as the story needs

Make a term understandable through its action, result or immediate significance. Define it explicitly when the reader needs that definition. Do not repeat a glossary explanation whenever the term reappears.

A novice's discovery can be narrated with the adult chef's accurate vocabulary, but the passage must preserve what the novice did and did not understand at the time. Technical hindsight must not make the younger self already knowledgeable about the lesson being learned.

### 9.2 Give transformation distinct stages

When transformation is the point, identify the relevant difference between before and after. Then choose which observations the reader actually needs.

Do not describe the same transformation three times through appearance, texture and a technical lecture if all three passages deliver the same information. Distinct stages might establish initial expectation, changed material state, and the narrator's response. They must add different things.

Once the technical purpose has been explained, the later tasting moment should contribute a reaction, discovery or consequence. It should not restart the explanation.

## 10. Sophisticated vocabulary without affectation

Do not limit the narrator to the simplest available word. Informality and intelligence are compatible. A well-chosen word can capture a distinction that otherwise requires an awkward phrase.

Use the word if its meaning is exact, its register fits, and the author could plausibly own it. If it looks selected to display the writer's reading, replace it with a more natural expression.

### 10.1 Judgement and standards

| Word | Useful distinction | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exacting | Demands close adherence to a standard | Does not automatically imply cruelty or unreasonable demands. |
| Meticulous | Takes detailed care | Does not establish anxiousness, fussiness or vanity. |
| Fastidious | Particularly attentive to detail, cleanliness or propriety; context determines the emphasis | More personally selective than a generic `careful`. |
| Perfunctory | Done as a duty with little care or interest | Do not use for work that is merely quick. |
| Pedantic | Overconcerned with small details or rules in a way being criticised | Do not use to belittle necessary precision. |
| Discerning | Makes informed distinctions | Support through the distinctions made; avoid empty praise. |
| Pretentious | Claims or displays greater importance, sophistication or merit than is justified | Describe the relevant display. Expense alone does not establish pretension. |
| Ostentatious | Conspicuously showy | Does not necessarily mean incompetent, fraudulent or tasteless. |
| Workmanlike | Competently practical, often without showiness | Can praise or faintly diminish; choose the intended implication. |
| Improvised | Made or arranged without the usual preparation or resources | Does not necessarily mean makeshift, unsafe or inept. |

### 10.2 Behaviour, feeling and hindsight

| Word | Useful distinction | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Taciturn | Habitually says little | Do not infer a habit from one quiet exchange. |
| Reticent | Reluctant to disclose or speak freely | Silence alone does not establish reluctance. |
| Laconic | Uses few words in expression | Often describes manner more accurately than presumed feeling. |
| Quizzical | Questioning or mildly puzzled in appearance or tone | Keep the observation separate from knowledge of another person's thoughts. |
| Terse | Brief in a way that may feel abrupt | Does not necessarily mean furious. |
| Sardonic | Dryly mocking, often with bitterness or scepticism | Use in analysis when useful; prose may demonstrate the effect without naming it. |
| Wry | Dryly humorous, often recognising an awkward or ironic aspect | Does not require a sneer or an insult. |
| Rueful | Regret or disappointment mixed with self-awareness | Do not substitute it for any unhappy recollection. |
| Chastened | Made more restrained or humble by correction or experience | Stronger than having received criticism; an effect must be established. |
| Mortified | Intensely embarrassed or ashamed | Do not use for a minor inconvenience unless the exaggeration is recognisably the author's. |
| Indignant | Angry at perceived unfairness or offence | Different from anger at being caught or inconvenienced. |
| Equivocal | Open to more than one interpretation or insufficiently clear | Does not automatically mean dishonest. |
| Disingenuous | Not straightforward or sincere in a way that suggests pretence | A claim about conduct or intention, requiring support. |
| Incongruous | Out of keeping with its surroundings or context | A precise way to name mismatch without pretending it is profound. |
| Disproportionate | Greater or smaller than the occasion warrants | Make the relevant comparison clear. |
| Precarious | Unstable or insecure | Do not apply to an ordinary uncertain moment just to add drama. |

### 10.3 Concrete and cultural range

Words such as `detritus`, `appellation`, `veracity`, `panache`, `ascetic`, `prosaic`, `unctuous` and `reverence` are available when their distinctions are useful. None is intrinsically more literary than `scraps`, `name`, `truth`, `flair`, `plain`, `rich` or `respect`.

Do not build every paragraph around an unusual word. Do not strip a precise word merely because it is unusual. The surrounding sentence should make it feel inevitable rather than displayed.

Cultural references must belong to the author. Do not borrow a musician, film, literary allusion, city reference or historical comparison from Bourdain to manufacture sophistication. Use the narrator's established associations or stay with the direct observation.

## 11. People, competence and power

Describe people through the relevant observed behaviour and the narrator's honestly framed response. A vivid adjective is not a substitute for knowing what someone did.

Keep these distinctions clear:

- A demanding chef is not necessarily a sadist.
- A quiet person is not necessarily contemptuous.
- A skilled cook is not necessarily serene in every circumstance.
- A humiliating experience does not prove that humiliation was someone's intention.
- A confident person is not automatically arrogant.
- An apprentice's mistake is not proof of habitual incompetence.

The narrator may make a blunt judgement. It should remain their judgement, with the degree of certainty and hindsight the account supports. Avoid upgrading a description into an allegation through a loaded noun or verb.

Do not weaken the author's edge through unnecessary politeness. Do not intensify that edge by inventing venom. The strongest judgement often depends on an exact description and a restrained concluding word.

Respect for craft should also have vocabulary. Use precision, repetition, timing, preparation, attention and consistency where the events show them. If the only available language is failure, frenzy and punishment, the lexical range is too narrow for this memoir.

## 12. Register by narrative situation

| Situation | Lexical emphasis | Adjustment required |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First encounter with a technique | Tangible objects, unfamiliar actions, incomplete understanding | Allow the adult narrator to name the process without giving the apprentice knowledge they did not yet have. |
| A practiced task | Economical working language and relevant distinctions | Do not re-explain what the narrator and reader already understand. |
| A service under pressure | Tasks, timing, spatial constraints and material consequences | Make pressure specific. Resist automatic battle imagery. |
| A calm demonstration | Controlled actions, material change and attention | Calm vocabulary can hold authority and fascination. Do not add a reprimand to create drama. |
| Correction or rebuke | Remembered words, actual error and established response | Do not increase volume, duration or cruelty through synonyms. |
| Food tasted or handled | Dominant physical qualities and the narrator's response | Choose the details that distinguish this encounter. Avoid menu copy. |
| Affection or loyalty | Familiar language and remembered acts | Do not force every act of care through an insult or culinary metaphor. |
| Domestic life | Local, practical and relational vocabulary | Let home have its own objects, pressures and warmth. |
| Travel or an unfamiliar place | Observable surroundings and limits of understanding | Describe what the narrator encountered, rather than performing cultural authority. |
| Pain or fatigue | The body part, task or ordinary movement affected | Do not invent symptoms to make the reader feel the suffering. |
| Retrospective reflection | Accurate distinctions, plain admissions and proportionate judgement | Avoid therapeutic, motivational or academic language the author has not chosen. |
| A ChefTip transition | The actual lesson emerging from the preceding experience | Preserve the narrative preparation and the tip's established wording and placement. |

The same word can succeed in one situation and fail in another. `Perfunctory` may clarify a retrospective judgement but sound false in a young apprentice's immediate spoken thought. `Fuck` may supply the exact beat in one sentence and dilute it in the next.

## 13. Profanity, irreverence and sexual observation

Profanity is a functional part of this voice. Preserve its actual work: emphasis, affiliation, disbelief, irritation, comic reversal or emotional exposure.

Apply these rules:

1. Do not add swearing to certify kitchen authenticity.
2. Do not remove swearing merely to make a sentence more elegant.
3. Check whether the swear word bears the stress the author intends. Moving it can change the beat.
4. Do not escalate one insult into a more hostile one for variety.
5. Keep character dialogue within its remembered or approved language. The narrator's vocabulary does not belong automatically to every speaker.
6. Judge repetition by effect. An intentional repeated curse can carry pressure; mechanically varying it can weaken the passage.

Attraction, glances and innuendo may be part of a scene when the author establishes them. Describe the actual observation at the scale it occupied. Do not enlarge a glance into prolonged scrutiny, turn a person into a catalogue of body parts, or attach a universal claim about a workforce to justify a particular memory.

Equally, do not insert an editorial apology or moral lecture the author has not asked for. Lexical accuracy means neither sanitising the observation nor inventing a more lurid one.

The reference's sexual language, group judgements and insults are features of that narrator's account. Their presence does not make them ingredients this memoir must reproduce.

## 14. Figurative language and double meaning

A comparison should make the subject easier to see, feel or understand, or express a judgement the literal wording cannot carry as effectively.

Before keeping it, test:

- Is the literal observation already accurate?
- Does the comparison fit the actual property being described?
- Does it belong to the author's associations and humour?
- Does it change the reader's understanding, or merely announce that a writer is present?
- Has another nearby comparison already done this work?
- Does it force a change of tone, rhythm or scale?

Prefer one coherent comparison to a string of images. Do not have a kitchen become a battlefield, circus, church and sinking ship within the same short passage unless the author has deliberately built that movement.

Treat stock military, religious and criminal imagery as options requiring a reason. Do not import a vocabulary of brigands, mercenaries or sacred rites merely because it appears in the reference.

### 14.1 Understatement

Understatement depends on the surrounding facts supplying the scale. A mild verdict can be forceful after a precise account. Do not enlarge it with an intensifier because it looks too simple in isolation.

### 14.2 A literal rule with a second meaning

If the author has established a double meaning, make the literal situation clear enough to earn it. Preserve the practical meaning of the rule and the human circumstance that complicates it.

Do not explain both readings several times. Do not add a final sentence translating the joke or announcing its cleverness. Do not anticipate the payoff so explicitly that the later line has nothing left to do.

## 15. Repetition, lexical economy and sound

### 15.1 Review four kinds of repetition

| Level | What to inspect | Appropriate remedy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Word or word family | Repeated adjectives, intensifiers, unusual words and variants such as `awkward` / `awkwardness` | Retain necessary usage; otherwise cut or recast for the intended meaning. |
| Explanation | The same technique, relationship or context taught again | Remove the second explanation unless it supplies a necessary new distinction. |
| Image | Reused metaphor, sensory image or stock description | Keep only if its return changes the image's significance. |
| Narrative meaning | Different sentences delivering the same judgement or lesson | Remove redundant work even if there are no repeated words. |

Review locally, across the chapter, and across the relevant preceding and following chapters when available. A paragraph can be clean in isolation and repetitive in sequence.

Words already flagged in the project's available guidance include `considerable`, `substantial`, `awkward` and `awkwardness`. Session context also flags repeated `remarkably` and generic social framing such as `our world`. These are attention points, not a universal ban list. Apply the underlying test to any recurrent word or construction.

Do not replace a repeated word with an arbitrary synonym. Ask what new information the sentence contributes. If it contributes none, a new adjective cannot rescue it.

### 15.2 Necessary repetition and deliberate return

Retain technical terms, names, dialogue patterns and purposeful repetition where they perform distinct work. An established motif can return when the later context gives it a new consequence or perspective. Mere rewording is not new meaning.

Do not flatten deliberate repetition into a machine-generated variety of terms. Do not defend redundant explanation as a motif unless its changed function can be identified.

### 15.3 Sound is part of lexical choice

Read neighbouring sentence endings together. Watch for accidental rhyme, jingles, repeated suffixes and repeated stresses that make the prose feel staged.

The author's stockpot feedback specifically rejects the successive pairing of `ridiculous` and `delicious` in that context. Neither word is generally prohibited. Repair the unwanted echo by changing only what is necessary within the authorised scope; do not dismantle a successful paragraph to avoid one sound.

Preserve intentional alliteration, repetition or roughness when it belongs to the author. Do not polish every sentence into the same smoothness.

## 16. Plain language, weak language and overworked language

These categories are different.

**Plain language** carries the necessary meaning without unnecessary display. It is often the right choice at emotional or comic turning points.

**Weak language** fails to identify what matters: vague praise, unspecified action, unsupported intensifiers or an explanation detached from the scene.

**Overworked language** adds properties, images or judgements after the passage has already delivered them.

Watch for these tendencies rather than enforcing mechanical bans:

| Tendency | Typical symptom | Editorial instruction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Promotional food description | `Exquisite`, `mouth-watering`, `culinary masterpiece` without distinguishing detail | Name the actual property or keep a direct personal verdict. |
| Generic atmosphere | `A symphony of aromas`, `a cacophony of chaos` | Identify the relevant source of smell or sound, if established. |
| Manufactured profundity | `It was more than food`, followed by a general life lesson | Ask whether the scene has already conveyed the meaning. Cut if it merely restates it. |
| Institutional narration | `Navigate challenges`, `demonstrate resilience`, `transformative journey` | Return to the person's actions and their own way of reflecting on them. |
| Excessive qualification | `Almost`, `somewhat`, `rather`, `quite` weakening an established condition | Remove only when certainty permits. Keep qualifiers that represent real uncertainty. |
| Adjective stacking | Several words describing the same richness, darkness or discomfort | Select the distinguishing property; preserve accumulation only if each item contributes. |
| Automatic synonym rotation | Changing the noun or judgement each time it returns | Preserve a stable referent and remove redundant meaning. |
| Unnecessary verbal force | Every action is violent or frantic | Restore the actual movement and degree of effort. |
| A sentence explaining its own joke | An extra clause or paragraph telling the reader why the preceding line is funny | End when the intended effect has landed. |

Do not replace `said` with an ornate dialogue tag. Do not turn `good` into `sublime` unless the narrator means sublime. Do not remove `fucking delicious` merely because it is a familiar judgement; determine whether it is the earned reaction at that point.

## 17. New demonstrations of lexical decisions

**All demonstrations below are newly written, hypothetical examples. They are not quotations from Bourdain, recovered memoir prose, approved chapter replacements or evidence that an event occurred. The stated facts belong only to each demonstration.**

### 17.1 Material precision without added flavour

**Given facts:** A sauce has a sheen and coats a spoon.

- Weak: “The sauce was incredibly rich and beautiful.”
- More exact: “The sauce had a sheen and coated the spoon.”
- Overreach: “The gelatin-rich sauce was silky with marrow and roasted garlic.”

The more exact version identifies two established properties. The overreach invents ingredients, a cause and another texture. The plain version can be sufficient; it does not require an additional metaphor.

### 17.2 An action carries the distinction

**Given facts:** The narrator removes surface fat with a ladle.

- Vague: “I worked carefully on the stock.”
- More exact: “I skimmed the fat from the stock with a ladle.”

`Skimmed` supplies the action. `Carefully` could return if the care itself matters and is supported, but it should not replace the action.

### 17.3 Retain a quiet verb

**Given facts:** Chef checks the seasoning and adds salt without speaking.

- Inflated: “Chef attacked the sauce, ruthlessly correcting its deficiencies.”
- More exact: “Chef checked the seasoning and added salt without saying anything.”

The quieter version preserves the known action. It leaves the surrounding scene to establish what the silence means instead of assigning aggression.

### 17.4 A richer word earns its place

**Given facts:** A check is performed merely to satisfy a requirement; little attention is paid.

- Diffuse: “He did the check because he had to, without paying much attention to it.”
- Possible compression: “The check was perfunctory.”

`Perfunctory` captures a specific distinction. Keep the longer version if its conversational movement or explicitness better serves the author's paragraph. Do not use the compressed version if the source establishes only speed.

### 17.5 Technical accuracy does not determine the emotional verdict

**Given facts:** The narrator tries unfamiliar food and enjoys it intensely; no specific flavour notes are recorded.

- Supported direct verdict: “It was fucking delicious.”
- Unsupported embellishment: “It tasted of browned butter, smoke and mineral sweetness.”

The direct verdict is more faithful. Missing sensory evidence cannot be supplied from genre expectation. A further interview question may produce detail; an adjective cannot substitute for that answer.

### 17.6 Repetition is not repaired by a synonym

**Given text:** “The room felt awkward. There was an uncomfortable silence. Nobody seemed at ease.”

These sentences may all perform the same job. Replacing `awkward` with `strained` does not add value. Keep the sentence that best expresses the intended observation and preserve any independently useful fact. Do not invent a gesture to justify the other two.

### 17.7 A glance is a glance

**Given facts:** Someone enters; the narrator briefly looks up.

- Faithful: “I glanced up when she came in.”
- Unwarranted escalation: “I watched her cross the room, unable to look away.”

The second version invents movement, duration and compulsion. More visual language has made the event less accurate.

### 17.8 A remembered act can carry affection

**Given facts:** A person leaves dinner covered for the narrator returning late.

- Abstract: “It was a profound demonstration of unconditional support.”
- Concrete: “She'd left my dinner covered on the bench.”

The concrete version allows context to carry the relationship. Do not add steam, the smell of a particular meal or the person's motive unless established.

### 17.9 Intensity changes the fact

**Given facts:** A pan is put down harder than usual; there is no record of damage.

- Supported: “He put the pan down hard.”
- Unsupported: “He hurled the pan across the kitchen.”

`Hurled` is not a vivid synonym for `put down`. It changes the event.

### 17.10 Character judgement needs the right distinction

**Given facts:** A chef insists on accurate portion sizes because consistency matters.

- Potentially accurate: “He was exacting about portions.”
- Different judgement: “He was pedantic about portions.”

The second sentence dismisses the attention to detail as excessive. That may contradict the narrator's respect for the standard. Select the judgement the author actually intends.

### 17.11 Preserve uncertainty

**Given facts:** The narrator hears that a colleague has been dismissed but has no confirmation.

- Faithful: “I'd heard he'd been sacked.”
- Inaccurate compression: “He'd been sacked.”

Removing the qualifier changes the narrator's evidence. Lexical economy does not justify it.

### 17.12 Explain an unfamiliar term through its use

**Given facts:** A cook places completed plates on a service counter for waitstaff to collect; that counter is called the pass in this workplace.

- Legible introduction: “I put the finished plates on the pass for the waiters to collect.”
- Unnecessary interruption: A separate paragraph defining the pass after that sentence.

The sentence makes the function clear. Later references can use `the pass` without restarting the explanation.

## 19. Working procedure: drafting, revision and assembly

### 19.1 Before writing

Read the current author instruction, the actual voice profile, the relevant source passage and the factual material for the scene. For a revision, identify what is open to change and what is protected. Do not assume every earlier draft is interchangeable.

Read the relevant entry and exit context when the task involves fitting a passage into a chapter. Read neighbouring chapters when the task requires a broader repetition or continuity check and they are available. State a concrete limitation if they are not; do not claim a full continuity review from an isolated extract.

### 19.2 Draft or revise the authorised material

Write from the established facts and narrative intention. Select vocabulary while building the sentence, so the language belongs to the thought. Consult the lexical distinctions in this profile when a particular word is uncertain; do not feed every sentence through a synonym exercise.

For an existing passage, use this order:

1. Retain language already doing its job.
2. Correct imprecision in the open text.
3. Strengthen nouns and verbs where there is a real gain.
4. Add or refine sensory detail only from established evidence.
5. Adjust judgement and register where the original misses the author's meaning.
6. Remove repetition and surplus explanation without deleting narrative function.
7. Read the resulting passage for voice, sound and continuity.

### 19.3 Assemble from the authoritative text

When asked to integrate an approved replacement, insert the replacement exactly and preserve surrounding text outside the authorised boundary. Do not improve unrelated sentences during assembly.

If the insertion exposes a genuine bridge problem, identify the exact transition and propose the minimum additional change when it falls outside scope. Where the author has already authorised bridge repair, perform it within that permission. Do not request approval again for work already authorised.

### 19.4 Record only useful editorial information

When review notes are requested or required by the project, record the meaningful lexical changes and why they were made. A compact table can identify the original phrase, proposed phrase, intended gain and any factual dependency.

Keep those notes outside the manuscript. Do not interrupt a requested complete chapter with commentary about word selection. Do not produce lengthy audit material unless it serves the task.

## 20. Acceptance criteria

### 20.1 Non-negotiable checks

The passage fails this profile if any answer below is no:

- **Factual fidelity:** Do all added or changed words preserve what happened, what was known and what remains uncertain?
- **Author authority:** Is protected text unchanged except where revision was authorised?
- **Voice fidelity:** Does the prose still sound like this author, with the intended rhythm, beat, humour and emotional stance?
- **Technical accuracy:** Do culinary terms identify the right objects, states and operations?
- **Semantic fidelity:** Have degree, intention, relationship, timing and judgement remained accurate?
- **Scope fidelity:** Has the work stayed within the requested drafting, revision or assembly task?

### 20.2 Quality checks

Assess these contextually; do not turn them into quotas:

| Dimension | A successful passage | A warning sign |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Specificity | The reader can identify the relevant thing, action or quality | Description could be moved unchanged to almost any kitchen |
| Range | Plain, technical and reflective language appear where useful | Every sentence is equally coarse, elevated or technical |
| Sensory value | Detail distinguishes the actual encounter | A stack of attractive adjectives without new perception |
| Economy | Each phrase adds information, pressure, response or necessary movement | The same point is made again in different words |
| Character | Vocabulary preserves individual people and the narrator's perspective | Loaded labels replace evidence |
| Sound | Word choice supports the author's cadence and landing points | Accidental rhyme, repeated stress or a conspicuously polished voice |
| Continuity | The passage adds to what precedes it and prepares what follows | Repeated groundwork or a payoff explained before it arrives |
| Restraint | The language stops at the right point | Every observation becomes a metaphor, judgement or lesson |

No score or checklist can overrule an author's precise correction. Word-frequency counts can flag possible repetition; they cannot establish that a paragraph works. Do not prescribe a number of rare words, swear words, sensory details or metaphors per page.

## 21. Compact operating directive

Use this block when a short instruction is needed to activate the full profile. It is an index to the rules above, not a replacement for reading them.

> Apply `vocabulary-context-profile.md` alongside the current author voice profile. Preserve the author's factual authority, latest corrections, settled wording, voice, rhythm and beat. Use exact nouns, accurate verbs, discriminating sensory language and a full but natural range of colloquial, technical and sophisticated vocabulary. Choose words for meaning and context, never for display. Do not invent details, escalate actions, attribute motives, transplant another writer's persona, or replace repetition with arbitrary synonyms. Keep necessary technical repetition; remove duplicated explanation and imagery. Check sentence sound, the surrounding paragraph and the relevant chapter bridges. Revise only within the authorised scope. If existing language already works, retain it.
