Director’s Cut — STARTING MONDAY

Chapter: STARTING MONDAY
Session date: 2026-09-03 to 2026-09-04
Manuscript status: Reconstructed and revised draft
Purpose: Private author development record — not for publication

────────

Where the chapter began

This chapter did not begin as a clean chapter. It began as a recovery
problem.

The manuscript already had the incarceration material and the later
culinary-career material, but the connective tissue between them had
been fragmented across older drafts and historical work under headings
including HIGH RISK OF REOFFENDING, THE WOMAN WHO CHOSE COOKING,
FIRST DAY and THE CLOWN SUIT. Some contained useful memories; some
contained superseded facts; some no longer belonged where they had
originally been placed.

One correction represented the standard applied throughout the session.
An old version had the narrator working in the prison kitchen. That was
wrong. The established recollection is that he was a wing cleaner with
Ivian, mopping floors and emptying bins, gaining extra unlock time and
additional milk portions. A prison-kitchen origin would have made a neat
culinary irony. It would also have been fiction, so it was rejected.

At first the missing chapter appeared to be about how the narrator ended
up in cooking school. By the end, that remained technically true, but it
was no longer enough. What emerged was a chapter about being made to
turn up before there was any internal reason to want to.

The parole office changed first

The recovered material supplied the mechanism: release, parole, a change
of parole officer, and enrolment in commercial cookery that was not the
narrator’s idea.

The new parole officer is remembered as Sarah or possibly Sharon. That
uncertainty remains. She was recalled as young, late twenties or around
the narrator’s age, and new to the role. She was not reconstructed as
frightened, aggressive or theatrically dominant. What emerged was firmer
and more credible: by-the-book, calculated, unflinching, and unable to
afford appearing fearful in a job dealing with parolees.

The narrator’s outward compliance was not acceptance. Being told exactly
what to do by a woman around his own age did not sit well with him, but
he understood the material power she held:

> She had the ability to send me straight back to prison.
> 
> I knew it.
> 
> She knew I knew it.

His answer—

> “Nah. All fucken good.”

—worked because the spoken words and internal state were almost
opposites.

Another factual correction changed the scene materially. The narrator
was not looking for legitimate work. He had no job, but money itself was
not the problem from his perspective. He knew how to make money through
the world he already understood, and his parole officer did not know
how.

That led to the distinction:

> She wasn’t really trying to fix the money.
> 
> She was trying to fix what I did with my time.

The intervention became about structure, attendance and accountability
rather than rescuing an unemployed young man who was unsuccessfully job
hunting.

Monday had to feel lived

The recovered FIRST DAY material held many of the right facts: 0630,
the clothes, BMX, cigarette, unfamiliar classroom, Glen, the handshake
and the confrontation.

The writing initially carried too much of the clipped rhythm that had
worked in the parole-office confrontation. There, short sentences
reflected pressure. Used continuously on Monday morning, they made the
memory feel assembled.

A central writing discovery followed: to create the sensation of being
teleported back into the memory, the prose needed ordinary physical
continuity rather than more dramatic description.

Getting dressed. Going to the shed. Smoking. Taking the BMX. Finding the
building. Leaving the bike outside. Finishing the cigarette. Eventually
walking in.

Stacey’s presence also gave the morning a lived social context. She did
not need a motivational speech. Her practical position could be reduced
to one word:

> Go.

The cones remained because sanitising them would falsify the point in
the narrator’s life being depicted. He smoked cannabis before leaving
because he was nervous. He had not been transformed before arriving at
culinary school.

The cigarette and sequencing

A small wording discussion became an important lesson in temporal
authenticity.

The observation that the cones had not settled the nerves originally
appeared after the action of standing outside. On rereading, the
narrator identified that the internal state belonged before the
behaviour it explained.

The final sequence compressed naturally around the recognised behaviour
of a smoker arriving somewhere uncomfortable, finishing the cigarette
before going inside and buying a little more time without needing to
announce that this is what he is doing.

The narrator noted that smokers—and people accustomed to being around
smokers—would recognise the sequence immediately.

The scene therefore did not need to explain that he was delaying entry
because he was nervous. The cigarette could show it.

Respect without retrospective virtue

The recovered confrontation contained a line about showing respect
because “other people actually want to learn.” That did not survive
scrutiny.

At this stage, the narrator did not want to be there and did not yet
care about cooking. Giving him an educational motive made him more
respectable but less truthful.

The line was reduced to:

> “Oi. Show some fucken respect.”

Respect itself was sufficient motivation.

That opened a larger character understanding. Cooking did not create the
narrator’s underlying moral code. Respect, loyalty, family, keeping
one’s word, protecting people, doing one’s part and not taking liberties
were already embedded. What changed over the memoir was not necessarily
the principle, but the repertoire available for expressing and enforcing
it.

In the classroom, he identified behaviour he genuinely regarded as
disrespectful. Challenging another student to step outside was
disproportionate by ordinary classroom standards, but internally
coherent with the world he understood.

The chapter therefore could not become a conventional rehabilitation
scene in which Glen teaches him what respect really means.

Glen redirected; he did not correct

This became one of the session’s most consequential wording decisions.

Glen’s recovered response was:

> “I appreciate the sentiment, but that’s not how we handle things in a
> classroom.”

An early formulation said Glen “corrected” the narrator. The narrator
challenged that premise. “Corrected” implied that the underlying
principle was wrong, while Glen’s own wording acknowledged the
sentiment.

The narrator proposed redirection.

The locked wording became:

> He didn’t make a big deal of it. Didn’t raise his voice.
> 
> Just redirected me.

That single word protected the character history. Glen was not replacing
the narrator’s morality. He was showing that the same standard could
operate through another mechanism.

The other student was also kept free of invented psychology.
Descriptions of fear, paleness or nervous laughter were unnecessary and
unsupported. Instead:

> His face changed.
> 
> He knew.

The reader was trusted to understand the social mechanics.

At break, the student stayed inside. The later narrator’s:

> Can’t imagine why.

allowed humour without turning the scene into apology, heroism or
self-condemnation.

The uniform and an accidental connection

The first practical sessions brought the chef uniform and the narrator’s
resistance to the hat.

The comedy was real, but a remembered detail about Glen added weight. On
practical days Glen also wore uniform: black pants, white chef’s jacket
with black buttons and the traditional tall chef’s hat. When moving
around the kitchen conducting class, the hat was on. When teaching from
the front, he took it off and placed it on the bench beside him.

The narrator specifically rejected language implying it was “obvious”
that Glen’s hat was taller or that the detail required explanation. The
observation was enough.

More importantly, Glen followed the standard he imposed.

That made the narrator’s objection harder to sustain without anyone
needing to explain a leadership lesson.

During this discussion an unplanned connection to the later
GOOSE & GANDER chapter became visible. Glen is an early example of
someone imposing a standard while visibly submitting to it himself. That
resonates with a later chapter in which unequal standards carry
substantial weight.

The session deliberately chose not to exploit the connection. No
explicit foreshadowing should be added, and GOOSE & GANDER should not
be altered to advertise it. The narrator considers that later chapter
one of the strongest in the memoir. The earlier factual detail simply
gives it more history when it arrives.

Refusing the origin myth

The chapter was close to becoming too responsible for the whole
culinary-school journey.

That direction was rejected.

The first practical did not need to reveal hidden talent. No knife
needed to become destiny. The narrator did not need to realise that
cooking was his calling.

He was there because Sarah put him there.

What changed was smaller:

> But I kept turning up.

The chapter closes on attendance:

> Theory days. Practical days. Tests.
> 
> Nine o’clock every morning.
> 
> At first, that was enough.

This deliberately withholds the later transformation. The achievement of
this chapter is not becoming a chef. It is turning up.

What the chapter unexpectedly unlocked

While reconstructing this period, the parole-office scene unlocked
memories about the narrator’s post-release living situation that had not
yet been properly integrated.

Established recollection from the session is that, on release, he was
living at Stacey’s mother’s place. Stacey had been staying there during
his incarceration, and this was the address he had hoped would be
approved for home detention.

The household included Stacey, her mother, a younger sister
approximately fifteen and a half-sister approximately nine. Those ages
remain approximate.

Within weeks the arrangement deteriorated as Stacey’s mother’s
on-and-off boyfriend—the younger half-sister’s father—began seeing
her again and spending time around the house.

The narrator recalled incidents in which he defended people he
considered family through violence, despite potentially risking his
parole and newly regained freedom.

A central contradiction became visible in discussion: he could act with
absolute loyalty toward people he regarded as family even when they did
not necessarily regard him as family themselves.

That material was deliberately not forced into STARTING MONDAY. The
incidents, chronology, triggers, actions, consequences and household
dynamics remain to be reconstructed from memory.

The narrator also recalled beginning to devise an exit plan for himself
and Stacey so they could establish their own place. At some stage, Ma
recognised that his commitment to cookery was genuine—that he was
actually sticking with it—and became involved in helping make that
next step possible. The timing and nature of that help remain to be
established.

This gives “kept turning up” the potential to acquire additional weight
later: culinary-school attendance was occurring alongside instability
that the completed chapter itself does not yet explain.

Directions deliberately rejected

The session rejected a series of narratively convenient but less
truthful directions.

The prison-kitchen origin was rejected because it was false. The
narrator was not made into a legitimate job seeker because he was not
one. The parole officer was not turned into a frightened young woman,
bully or inspirational rehabilitator. The narrator was not made grateful
for enrolment.

The classroom confrontation was not justified by pretending he already
cared about learning. The other student’s emotions were not invented.
Glen was not made into the man who taught him morality. “Corrected me”
was rejected because it changed the premise; “redirected me” preserved
it.

The uniform was not over-symbolised. Glen’s seniority was not
editorialised through his hat. The GOOSE & GANDER connection was not
converted into deliberate foreshadowing. The first practical was not
transformed into revelation.

Most importantly, the chapter was not allowed to race forward into later
success simply because the present-day narrator knows that success
eventually came.

Where STARTING MONDAY arrived

At the outset, the chapter seemed to answer a chronological question:
how did a young man leave prison and end up in culinary school?

By the end, its emotional centre was more precise.

Sarah did not give him a vocation. She gave him somewhere he had to be.

Glen did not remake his values. He demonstrated another way those values
could operate.

The uniform did not make him a chef. He put it on partly because the
standard applied to everyone, including the man enforcing it.

Cooking did not immediately become identity.

He returned the next day.

And then kept returning.

The reader can see that something has begun, but the younger narrator is
not required to understand what it will become. Hindsight knows the
importance of Monday. The man living Monday did not.

The chapter now permits both truths to coexist.

Unresolved matters

The parole officer’s name remains uncertain: Sarah or possibly Sharon.

Precise chronology around release, reporting and course commencement may
still require corroboration.

The living arrangement at Stacey’s mother’s house requires dedicated
reconstruction. Household composition and approximate ages should be
confirmed where possible.

The boyfriend’s return, the incidents involving protection and violence,
their triggers, participants, witnesses, police involvement, injuries,
dialogue and consequences must not be invented. They remain material for
the next interview.

The emotional contradiction of protecting people considered family while
not necessarily being regarded as family by them emerged during this
session, but the next reconstruction must distinguish what the narrator
understood then from what is visible now.

The timing and nature of Ma’s help remain unresolved.

The next chapter remains untitled. THE CLOWN SUIT is retired as a
prospective title and remains only a historical recovered-source
heading.

Later material concerning course achievement, City & Guilds distinction
and GRANT’S KITCHEN remains protected for its own place in the
manuscript.

Final reflection

Perhaps the clearest pattern in this session was how often the chapter
became stronger when something was removed.

A false culinary origin. A respectable job-search story. An explanation
for why respect mattered. An invented interpretation of another person’s
fear. A moral lesson from Glen. A dramatic first practical. An obvious
piece of foreshadowing.

Each would have organised the story more neatly.

None was necessary.

What remained was more contradictory and more recognisable: a
nineteen-year-old who could smoke cones because he was properly nervous
about walking into a classroom, threaten another student over manners
once he got there, object to a chef’s hat as though that were finally
the unreasonable part of the situation, and still recognise when another
man set a boundary without disrespecting him.

The person remembering these events can now see patterns the person
living them could not have named: standards, consistency, loyalty,
structure, the difference between correction and redirection, and the
significance of simply turning up.

But the person on that Monday morning did not know he was entering the
beginning of a career.

He was trying not to get breached.

The work of the session was therefore partly an exercise in keeping
three people distinct: the person who lived it, the person who remembers
it, and the person now trying to write it without allowing knowledge of
the ending to rewrite the beginning.

STARTING MONDAY now sits somewhere between them.

That is probably why it finally feels like the truth.
