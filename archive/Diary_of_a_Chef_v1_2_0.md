# DIARY OF A CHEF
## Restructured Draft v1.2.0 — December 2025

---

# CHICKEN AND MASH

It was 4 p.m. on a summer afternoon when I arrived at the vineyard restaurant.

Perched on a hill. Rows of green vines. Sprawling garden. Sky bright blue, not a cloud in sight. The kind of day that makes you feel like New Zealand is the best place on earth.

The kitchen was immaculate.

Highly organised. Clean in a way that only professional kitchens are. And the smell—rich, deep, almost sweet. Beef stock simmering somewhere in the back.

I was met by the sous chef.

They called him Fat Sam.

Broad, solid, rounded stomach, rosy red cheeks that made him look jolly until you heard him speak. Voice low, abrupt. Firm. He moved quickly for a man his size, and in near silence.

Unnerving.

> *"You the trial?"*

"Yeah."

> *"Get changed. Locker room's through there."*

---

I changed into my whites. Black Blundstones. Blue-and-white checked pants. White jacket. White apron. Polished my steel cap. Tied my apron.

Walked back into the kitchen.

Immediately got in the way.

I didn't know the layout. Didn't know the systems. Didn't know where anything was. The team was prepping for service, moving like a machine, and I was already a wrench in the gears.

> *"You hungry?"*

Fat Sam asks as he pushes me out of his way using only his size.

"Uh—"

> *"I'll get you something."*

At the time, I just thought he was being nice.

I realised years later and in hindsight, that he just wanted me out of his way and keep me occupied until Chef returned.

---

He plated me a meal.

Simple.

Bone-in chicken supreme. Mashed potatoes. Asparagus. Chicken jus.

I stood at the steel prep bench in the back half of the kitchen, looking out the window into a staff-only courtyard. Sky still bright blue. I could hear the first tickets being called in the distance. The sound of protein hitting a hot pan. The low hum of the dishwasher.

I cut into the chicken.

Almost pearlescent white. Glossy in texture. I'd never seen chicken like this before.

The jus was crystal clear and thin. Barely clinging to the plate.

The asparagus were peeled and vibrant green. The mash was velvet smooth.

I took a little of each component on my fork.

Put it in my mouth, it stopped me dead mid-chew.

---

Complete euphoria. Excitement. A small amount of fear.

I mentally checked out entirely.

Staring out the window for what felt like minutes.

I was hit with an internal monologue, a rolling sea of rhetorical questions:

*Will I get this job?*

*How can chicken taste like this?*

*I'll never get this job.*

*What is this sauce?*

*I have to get this job.*

*Remember to breathe.*

*Contain the excitement.*

Fat Sam popped his head around the corner.

> *"You like your dinner?"*

*Someone is talking to you.*

It took me a moment to register where I was. What the fuck was happening.

*Someone is talking to you.*

*You need to answer.*

"I have to learn to cook like this," I said.

He laughed. Short, sharp.

> *"It's just chicken and mash, mate. You'll learn a lot more than that here."*

---

I couldn't believe what I was experiencing. But Sam was right.

It was just chicken and mash.

But how?

---

By the time I finished, Chef had arrived.

We stood outside while he smoked. Talked about food and kitchens and what I wanted to learn. I didn't work that service, but I stayed and watched.

Part deer in the headlights.

Part kid on Christmas morning.

At the end of the night, he offered me the job.

I said yes.

---

The drive home took an hour. I don't remember any of it.

Just the stupid grin I couldn't wipe off my face. The steering wheel under my hands. The feeling that for once—for the first fucking time—I knew exactly where I was going.

Not because of the job offer. Not because of the vineyard or the bright blue sky or even the chicken—though I still think about that chicken.

For the first time in my life, I had a direction. A path I'd chosen—not been forced into, not stumbled onto, not assigned by a parole officer with a dying pot plant and a golden retriever on her desk.

This was mine.

And I was going to chase it with everything I had.

I didn't know yet what that would cost. What I'd give up. What I'd miss. The birthdays and anniversaries and ordinary Tuesday nights that would slip past while I stood over a hot stove, chasing something I couldn't quite name.

I didn't know any of that.

I was young. Invincible. Certain that there would always be more time.

# EARNING THE RIGHT

There's a moment when you stop being the problem.

No one tells you it's happened. There's no ceremony. No one pulls you aside and says *congratulations, you're not a liability anymore*. It just happens—slowly, invisibly—until one day you realise you're not waiting to be told what to do. You just know. And you do it.

Months passed like that.

Services blurred into each other. Prep lists got shorter—not because there was less to do, but because I could do it faster. The panic that used to rise in my chest when the docket rail started filling? Gone. Replaced by something else entirely—a tightness, an electricity, the kind of nervous energy that sharpens everything instead of blurring it. The rush. The thing you can't explain to anyone who hasn't felt it. The reason people stay.

I learned things I didn't know I was learning.

How to read a kitchen by sound alone—the rhythm of the pans, the pitch of Chef's voice, the silence that meant someone was in the shit. How to move through a crowded pass without touching anyone, without breaking the flow. How to taste something and know, instantly, what it needed—more acid, less salt, another minute on the heat.

None of it came from a textbook. It came from repetition. From fucking up and fixing it. From watching people who were better than me and absorbing everything I could.

---

The vineyard kitchen had a way of stripping you down.

Not cruelly—though it could feel that way in the moment. Just... honestly. You couldn't hide in a kitchen that small, with standards that high. Every weakness got exposed. Every gap in your knowledge got found. And either you filled those gaps or you left.

A lot of people left.

I watched them go—the ones who couldn't handle the hours, the ones who wilted under pressure, the ones who thought they wanted this life until they actually lived it. Some lasted a few months. Some lasted days. One guy made it to his third service, walked out mid-ticket, and never came back for his knife roll.

I understood. I did.

This job asks for everything. Your time, your body, your relationships, your sleep. It takes and takes and the only thing it gives back is the work itself—the chance to do it again tomorrow, maybe a little better than you did it today.

For some people, that's not enough.

For me, it was becoming everything.

It was everything.

---

I think about those early days and the lessons learnt sometimes.

Not with nostalgia—kitchens don't really allow for that. More like... recognition. The understanding that everything I am now started in that kitchen. Every instinct, every standard, every opinion I hold about what food should be and how a brigade should run—it all traces back to those fluorescent lights, that relentless heat, those people who taught me without ever calling it teaching.

Chef, who could end a conversation with a look and whose silence was the closest thing to approval you'd ever get. Three years I worked under him. I could count the compliments on one hand and still have fingers left.

Marco, quiet and methodical, who could break down a whole animal faster than anyone I've ever seen and never once made it look impressive.

The dishies, holding the whole operation together from the back corner, never getting the credit they deserved.

---

Over the years, I've collected lessons.

Some from Chef. Some from Sam. Some from burnt fingers and broken bodies and nights I should've walked away but didn't.

I call them ChefTips.

They're numbered but not by when I learned them. By how much they cost. The higher the number, the cheaper the lesson.

So here they are. Ten ChefTips.

I'll even give you one for free you're gonna need it if you want to survive this industry.

---

# SET UP YOUR STATION

Before we get into the expensive ones, I'm going to give you something for free.

Call it a welcome gift. Call it the first thing every apprentice learns before they learn how quickly they can fuck everything up. Call it the difference between looking like you belong and looking like someone's nephew who got hired as a favour.

This one won't cost you anything except maybe a bit of pride when you realise you've been doing it wrong your whole life.

---

## ChefTip #11

*"Put a damp tea towel or cloth under a chopping board—it will stop it sliding around."*

This was the first thing I ever learnt in a commercial kitchen. (Directly followed by being shown that "cunt" is a way to express fondness and admiration.) How to set up a chopping board. Simple. So, so simple.

This is quite a significant point of my life as a Shit Kicker. The first ever minute I was in a kitchen, the Chef asked me to set up a board and when I pulled it onto the bench he said:

"Chuck a tea towel under it—it won't slide."

Since then, I have never set up a chopping board differently. Use it, cherish it, love it. It's a fucking great tip.

You're weeeelcome.

---

But what about ChefTip Number One?

That one cost me a lot.

You'll get to it. But not yet.

First, you need to meet the man who made sure I survived long enough to learn anything at all.

# FAT SAM

There's a version of me that most were unaware of. One I was trying to move on from.

Most people who've worked alongside me have no idea that version ever existed. And that's by design.

Something I learned over the years is that a kitchen is the only place where no one gives a fuck about your past. You find school dropouts, misfits, immigrants—some of which may or may not be legal—ex-criminals. None of it matters.

As long as you can cook.

I'm not the social type. Avoided small talk like it was the plague. Didn't want anyone knowing about my past.

So I never gave them the chance to ask.

---

"You know me better than my wife at this point. It's time for you to move on."

Chef said it like he was handing me a docket. Matter-of-fact. No ceremony. Just the truth, delivered the way kitchens deliver everything—fast, blunt, and with the assumption you'll figure out how to handle it.

Two and a half years.

Two and a half years of punishment in a room hotter than Satan's arsehole, with fluorescent lights that made everyone look like they were already dead, and a sous chef whose entire purpose on this earth seemed to be making sure I understood—daily, hourly, by the fucking minute—that I wasn't good enough yet.

And now, apparently, I was.

CDP.

Chef de Partie. My own section. Responsibility that actually meant something.

Three and a half years earlier, I'd been living a different life entirely. The kind that doesn't go on a résumé. The kind that ends in one of two places, and neither of them involves a starched apron and a nine-inch Wüsthof.

Everyone in that kitchen was blissfully unaware of who I was... who I used to be?

Except Fat Sam.

---

Fat Sam found out about three months after I started. I never asked how. Didn't matter. The moment he knew, something shifted—not in how he treated me, but in *why*.

See, Sam was trained in the days before HR sanitised the industry. Back when kitchens were built on fear and repetition and a kind of violence that was considered educational. He was Chef's enforcer. Built like a commercial freezer and twice as cold. For a man that size, he was disturbingly quiet—you'd swear the floorboards should've groaned under him, but they never did. He just appeared. Materialised at your shoulder the exact moment your knife slipped or your timing went to shit.

And once he knew my history?

He rode me harder than anyone else on the line.

At first, I thought it was a power play. Some fucked-up territorial nonsense. Here's the guy with a past, let's see how long until he snaps.

It took me a long time to realise it wasn't that at all.

---

There was one afternoon—middle of prep, the kind of day where the air is already thick and the first ticket hasn't even dropped yet—and Sam was on me about something. I don't even remember what. Could've been my knife work. Could've been how I was storing the mise. Could've been the way I was fucking breathing.

Didn't matter.

What mattered was the feeling building in my chest. That old, familiar heat. The one I used to let out whenever someone pushed too hard, said the wrong thing, looked at me in a way that my old brain interpreted as *threat*.

Three and a half years earlier, this moment would've ended differently.

Three and a half years earlier, I wouldn't have let a bloke talk to me like this for more than a sentence before reminding him—physically, permanently—that I wasn't someone whose patience you tested.

Temper climbing. Vision narrowing. That old operating system booting up like it had never been switched off.

Mid-bollocking, I turned.

Looked him dead in the eye.

"Watch your fucken mouth before I stab you, cunt."

The kitchen didn't stop. That's the thing. Pans kept clanging. Someone was breaking down a fish two benches over. The extractor fan hummed its endless, indifferent hum. Nobody even glanced our way.

Sam didn't flinch.

Didn't blink.

Just held my eyes like he'd been waiting for this exact moment since the day he found out who I was.

"Go home," he said.

"Get fucked. I've got shit to do before service."

"Then go for a cigarette. Now. Cool off."

"I said I've got—"

"Cigarette. *Now.*"

We stayed like that for a beat. Maybe two. Eyes locked, neither of us giving an inch—but something in the air had shifted. There was no anger in his voice. No fear either. Just... understanding.

Like he'd seen this movie before.

Like maybe, a long time ago, he'd been the one standing where I was standing.

The rest of the brigade carried on around us. Oblivious or indifferent, I couldn't tell. Maybe both.

I wasn't about to go home. Call it stubbornness. Call it a sadistic attachment to the punishment. Call it not knowing how to walk away from something I'd actually chosen, for once in my miserable life.

But I went outside for a smoke anyway.

---

The alley behind that kitchen smelled like grease traps and old cardboard. I leaned against the wall, lit a cigarette, and tried to figure out what the fuck had just happened.

I'd threatened to stab a man.

In a room full of witnesses.

And instead of firing me, calling the cops, or putting me through a wall, he'd told me to take a break.

That's when it started to click.

Sam wasn't riding me because he wanted to break me. He was riding me because he knew—better than anyone—what I was capable of becoming if someone didn't. The version of me that walked into that kitchen wasn't built for this industry. Wasn't built for anything legal, really. And every time he pushed, every time he tested the edges of my patience, he was teaching me something I'd never learned anywhere else.

How to take it.

How to turn that heat into fuel instead of fire.

How to stay in the room when every instinct screams *leave* or *destroy*.

I finished the cigarette. Went back inside. Found Sam at his station, not even looking up.

I didn't apologise. He didn't expect one.

We just got back to work.

# THE ONION WILL NOT WAIT FOR YOU

There's a very specific moment in every young cook's life when the kitchen stops being this sexy, Gordon Ramsay-lit fantasy and starts becoming what it actually is: a pit of chaos, heat, and self-inflicted humiliation. For me, that moment arrived the day I realised an onion doesn't give a single shit about my personal wellbeing.

You learn that quickly.

Usually while crying—not from the onion, but from sheer existential despair because Chef is behind you asking why the fuck you're still chopping like you're in a home economics class with your Year Nine crush watching.

It was a Friday lunch service.

The kind where the entire brigade is vibrating with that special brand of panic you only see in trauma documentaries. I'm at my station trying to dice onions like a human metronome—consistent, clean, tidy—the sort of thing that would make a French culinary school instructor cry tears of joy. Instead, I'm producing chunks that look like they've been taken hostage and tortured for information.

Chef walks past, stops dead, and says:

"Are you... are you negotiating with the onion? Cut it. Don't interrogate it."

Right. Noted.

What he failed to mention was how fucking fast I was meant to be doing this. Because while I'm over here delicately coaxing the layers apart like it's some sort of emotional support vegetable, the rest of the kitchen is moving like choreographed violence. Pots slamming, timers screeching, someone yelling "HEY, WHO PUT THE FISH IN THE OVEN WITHOUT SETTING A BLOODY TIMER?" and the grill cook threatening to quit because he's out of jalapeños again.

And I'm still—still—halfway through onion number two, feeling like a fraudulent sack of shit in an apron.

Here's the thing though:

Once you've chopped ten thousand onions, you stop caring about tears. You stop caring about the sting, the fumes, the little microscopic cuts on your fingers. You stop caring that you've diced so much allium that your very being now smells like Bolognese base.

The onion becomes an extension of your soul.

A pungent, judgemental orb of truth.

Which brings me to today's lesson.

---

## ChefTip #10

*"Square your damn ingredients before you chop. Always."*

If you want uniform dice, fast slices, or anything that doesn't look like a toddler's craft project, you square the ingredient first.

You trim the root. You trim the tip. You create flat edges. You give the vegetable stability—structure—a sense of purpose. Because if it's rolling around on the board like a drunk uncle at Christmas, guess who it's taking down with it?

You. Your fingers. Your dignity.

This was the second thing I ever learned in a commercial kitchen, right after the sacred tea-towel-under-the-board revelation. Chef showed me once—once—and then walked off as if I'd just been handed the keys to the universe. Ever since then, every onion, carrot, leek, and sad excuse of a zucchini has been squared within an inch of its life.

It makes you faster. Cleaner. More consistent.

And consistency is the only thing separating us from the lunatics who cook one good meal a month and then tell us they "could've been a chef, you know."

Could you, Darren? Could you really?

You cried cutting one shallot at Christmas and had to sit down.

Anyway.

Square your ingredients. Square your life while you're at it.

Because unlike you, the onion isn't waiting.

# NEVER TAKE YOUR EYES OFF THE STOCKPOT

There's this moment somewhere between your first real burn and the day you finally stop flinching when someone shouts "Behind!"—when you realise the kitchen isn't simply a workplace. It's a living thing. Loud. Unpredictable. Slightly unhinged. And it has absolutely no interest in accommodating your feelings.

I'd just graduated from "onion hostage negotiator" to someone Chef trusted enough to give a job with actual consequences. Which, as it turns out, was its own kind of trap! because the second a chef believes you're competent, that's when they start handing you tasks fully capable of ruining your entire week.

He gestures toward this monstrous stockpot simmering on the back burner.

The thing looked like it contained the remains of a medieval feast. Bones, herbs, scraps, steam swirling up like it was exhaling old secrets.

"Keep an eye on that," he says.

I nod, pretending I'm ready, while internally acknowledging that I am absolutely not built for the level of responsibility that pot required. But that's the thing about kitchens: they don't wait for you to feel ready. They assume readiness is something you'll discover in the aftermath.

For the first few minutes, I'm hyper-attentive.

Watching the surface ripple. Listening to the gentle murmur of boiling stock. Feeling almost... capable?

Then service starts tightening around us like a vise.

The grill cook is losing his temper about ticket times. The pastry chef is whispering threats to a tray of soufflés. Someone down the line has dropped a full container of béchamel and is contemplating their life choices.

And in the middle of this swirling madness, I turn—just for a heartbeat—because someone yelled my name with the exact tone people usually reserve for news of a death or a pregnancy.

When I face the pot again, it's too late.

The damn thing is overflowing.

Not a gentle spill—a full-blown uprising. Foam marching down the sides. A slow, golden flood taking the express route across the stovetop and onto the floor.

All I can think is:

*So this is it. This is how careers end—drowning in poultry-flavoured regret.*

Chef appears behind me, silent at first. That's always worse.

Then, in that low voice chefs use right before they decide whether to yell or laugh:

"You turned your back on it, didn't you?"

And here's the most painful part:

He wasn't angry. He was disappointed.

Which, in kitchen terms, is borderline devastating.

---

## ChefTip #9

*"Never turn your back on a stockpot. If you're responsible for it, you're present for it."*

A pot doesn't wait for your attention.

It doesn't care about incoming dockets or your rising panic or the grill cook's latest meltdown.

It demands presence—real presence—the kind you can't fake.

Here's what "keeping an eye on it" actually means: You skim it every ten to fifteen minutes. Every time you pass, you glance. You know where the heat is set, and you check it hasn't crept up. You never assume "it was fine five minutes ago" means it's fine now. And if you have to leave your station, you hand it off—verbally, out loud, to a specific person who acknowledges the handoff.

Stock isn't just liquid—it's hours of work and the foundation of every sauce, jus, and braise that comes out of that kitchen. Let it boil over, and you're not just cleaning a stovetop. You're diluting flavour, losing body, and telling Chef that his time means less to you than whatever distraction pulled you away.

Stay with it. Stay connected. Stay aware.

Because the kitchen will test your attention long before it rewards it.

# HOT TRAYS DON'T ANNOUNCE THEMSELVES

There are moments in a chef's early life that permanently alter the chemistry of their nervous system.

Not the loud disasters—the dropped plates, the butchered mise en place, the burnt stock that smells like a small barn fire.

No, the ones that change you are quieter, more intimate. The mistakes you make when no one's watching... until suddenly everyone is.

My moment came courtesy of a roasting tray.

It was a Saturday service—the kind that makes even the veterans walk in looking like they've already lived eight lives before noon. The air had that particular buzz to it, the kind that means customers are out in force and the whole brigade is about to be dragged across the coals of expectation.

I was in charge of prepping confit duck legs, which is basically culinary gold.

Hours of cooking. Perfectly rendered fat. The kind of thing diners brag about on their drive home, pretending they understand craftsmanship because they once watched a cooking show finale.

I'd brought the tray out of the oven, set it on the bench, and stepped away for a second to grab more herbs. Not even a full step—just a half-turn, enough to break eye contact with the universe.

That's all it took.

Something about the kitchen's rhythm that day felt off-balance, like the room had tilted a few degrees without warning. Someone yelled something down the line—I didn't catch the words, but the tone told me it wasn't good. Someone else banged a pot. A timer started screaming like it had personal issues with us.

And in the chaos, I reached for the tray.

Not a gloved hand. Not a damp cloth. Just my bare, hopeful, freshly naïve fingers.

The pain wasn't sharp—it was immediate. Instant. Biblical.

It rocketed up my arm and straight into my bloodstream as if my nervous system had been waiting for this exact moment to initiate the rite of passage known as "Welcome to the industry, kid."

I didn't drop the tray—which honestly shocked me more than the burn.

Instead, I froze. Completely still. Like a possum in headlights but with significantly less dignity.

Chef looked over with that expression that lives somewhere between concern and disbelief.

"What did you grab?" he asked.

And all I could muster was:

"...Heat."

He didn't yell. He didn't swear.

He just nodded with this grim, almost affectionate acknowledgement—the kind senior chefs give you when they realise you've just learned one of the painful truths they were never going to bother explaining.

Because in kitchens, some lessons aren't taught. They're inherited through suffering.

Later, while I was wrapping my hand in the kind of bandage job that looked more like modern art than first aid, I realised something important:

In the kitchen, nothing warns you before it hurts you.

Hot trays don't glow red. Handles don't politely inform you they've been sitting in the oven for forty minutes. The pan you set down five seconds ago can somehow be hotter than the sun.

The kitchen will test your reflexes long before it respects them.

---

## ChefTip #8

*"Always check before you touch anything. Heat is patient—and it waits for idiots."*

Tap the tray with the corner of your cloth. Hover your hand above the handle. Ask.

Assume nothing.

Because burns aren't just physical—they're emotional.

The shame hurts longer than the skin ever will.

And every chef, every single one, has that one scar they earned not through bravery or skill, but through a split-second lapse in awareness.

That scar becomes part of your story.

A reminder that the kitchen demands respect—not fear, not arrogance, but presence.

You get sharper. Faster. More aware. More in tune with the rhythm of the room.

You start to feel the heat before you see it. Hear a mistake before it happens. Sense danger before anyone else notices it.

It's not magic. It's survival.

And eventually, survival becomes instinct

# THE ONES WHO STAY

Nobody warns you about the quiet parts.

The industry loves to talk about the noise—the screaming, the heat, the chaos, the adrenaline. That's the sexy stuff. That's what makes it onto Netflix and into the fantasies of people who think "chef" means white coat and tasting spoons and someone filming you being dramatic about a soufflé.

But no one tells you about 11:47pm on a Tuesday, sitting on an upturned milk crate in the alley, knees screaming, feet numb, watching steam rise off your own forearms because you've been standing over heat so long your body doesn't know how to cool down anymore.

That's the part that decides who stays.

---

The first time I properly understood kitchen humour, I thought I was witnessing a crime.

It was maybe three months in. I'd graduated from "liability" to "tolerable"—which, in kitchen terms, is basically a promotion. Still at the bottom of the ladder, but at least no one actively flinched when I walked past the pass.

We'd had a brutal Friday. One of those nights where the rail never emptied and every docket seemed to come with a modification designed by someone who hated joy. Dairy-free but with butter. Medium rare but not pink. "Chef's choice" followed by a list of seventeen things they didn't want.

Somewhere around the two-hundredth cover, one of the senior chefs—a guy named Marco who'd been on grill for six years and had forearms like industrial cables—cut himself.

Not badly. Just a slip. The kind of nick you'd bandage at home and complain about for three days. In the kitchen, he didn't even look down. Just grabbed a blue bandage from the kit, wrapped it tight with his teeth holding one end, and kept flipping steaks.

Blood was already seeping through by the time plates went up.

And the first thing—the first thing—anyone said was the guy on fish, without looking up from his pan:

"Finally. Some actual fucking flavour from your section."

The whole line laughed.

Marco included.

That's when I realised: this isn't cruelty. This is survival.

---

The best chefs I've ever worked with were also the meanest.

Not cruel. Mean. There's a difference.

Cruel people want to hurt you. Mean people want to sharpen you.

They'll call you an idiot for leaving your tongs in the wrong spot. They'll mock your knife work in front of the entire brigade. They'll ask, loudly, if you learned to season food in a hospital cafeteria.

And then, at the end of service, they'll stay an extra hour to show you how to break down a fish properly because they noticed you were struggling earlier and didn't say anything.

They won't tell you they're helping. They'll just be there, beside you, hands moving, voice low, explaining things they could easily leave you to figure out yourself.

"You're cutting against the grain. Feel that? That's wrong. Go with it. Slower. There."

---

There was one afternoon—maybe eight months in—when Sam showed me who he actually was.

He had this way of appearing behind you at the exact moment you were about to fuck something up. Not after. During. Like he had a sixth sense for incompetence.

"What are you doing."

Not a question. A statement. A verdict.

I spent the first few months convinced he hated me. That he'd been assigned to watch the apprentice fail so he could report back with evidence of my uselessness.

Then one night, I came in wrecked.

Not physically—though that too. I'd had one of those days where everything outside the kitchen was falling apart. Relationship stuff. The kind of thing you're supposed to leave at the door but can't always manage to.

I thought I was hiding it. I wasn't.

Halfway through prep, Sam appeared at my station. Didn't say anything. Just watched me butcher a brunoise that should have been uniform and was instead looking like gravel from a construction site.

I waited for the "what are you doing."

Instead, he reached over, took the knife from my hand, and said:

"Go smoke. Five minutes. I've got this."

That was it.

No follow-up. No "are you okay." No HR-mandated check-in about my emotional wellbeing.

Just: go, I've got this.

When I came back, my station was immaculate. Brunoise done. Mise reset. Like nothing had happened.

He never mentioned it again.

Neither did I.

But I'd take a bullet for that man.

---

That's the thing about kitchen loyalty.

It doesn't look like anything you'd recognise from normal life.

It's not hugs and heart-to-hearts and supportive text messages. It's not team-building exercises and trust falls and someone asking how your weekend was.

It's actions.

The dishie who sees you drowning and quietly starts running your pans back faster without being asked.

The guy on fish who calls his timings thirty seconds early because he clocked that you're behind and doesn't want Chef to notice.

The pastry chef who hates everyone and everything but somehow always has a spoon of something sweet waiting for you at the end of a shit service because she knows you skipped lunch and dinner and possibly breakfast too.

The head chef who screams at you for twenty minutes over a mistake that cost the restaurant twelve dollars, and then quietly takes the next major bollocking from the owner himself so it doesn't land on you.

Nobody says "I've got your back."

They just... have it.

---

I think about why we stay.

The hours are inhumane. The pay is laughable. The toll on your body is permanent. Your social life dies the moment you accept your first full-time position. Relationships crumble. Sleep becomes a rumour. Your feet develop their own weather system.

And yet.

There was a night last winter—one of those marathon services where everything went right and everything hurt and by the end we were so tired we could barely speak.

I was wiping down my station. The kitchen was finally quiet. Chef was doing his walk-through, the way he always does, checking everything's locked down before we go.

He stopped at my section. Didn't say anything for a moment. Just looked at the bench, the mise containers stacked and labelled for tomorrow, the cloth folded exactly where it should be.

Then he said:

"You're starting to look like you belong here."

That was it.

He walked off. I finished wiping down. We locked up. I went home and slept like the dead.

But that sentence—six words, no fanfare, probably already forgotten by the time he said them—that's the thing I carry.

Not because it was praise.

Because it was recognition.

From someone who'd seen me fuck up a hundred times and kept me anyway. Who'd watched me burn myself, humiliate myself, send out food I should've tasted and hadn't, and still trusted me with the next service.

That's why we stay.

Not for the food. Not for the money. Not for the dream of owning our own place one day.

For the moments when someone who's seen your worst decides you're worth keeping.

---

At the end of every service, there's a ritual.

Someone calls time. The grill goes off. The last plates hit the pass. We wipe down stations, scrub surfaces, return the room to the pristine state it was in before we spent six hours trying to destroy it.

And then we leave.

One by one, out into the night, back to lives that feel oddly small compared to what just happened.

But before we go, there's always a moment.

A nod. A tap on the shoulder. A muttered "good service" from someone too tired to say more.

Tomorrow we'll do it again.

And again.

And again.

Because that's what we do.

# IF YOU DIDN'T TASTE IT, IT ISN'T YOURS

There's a particular kind of shame that only happens in a kitchen.

Not the "oops, I messed up" kind.

The kind where you feel your insides fold in on themselves while you still have to keep moving because service doesn't give a shit about your spiritual crisis.

Mine arrived the first time I sent a sauce I hadn't tasted.

It was a midweek dinner service, one of those nights that pretends to be gentle at the start. A couple of early tables, a quiet rail, the illusion that maybe we'd get out before midnight and remember what outside air feels like.

By seven-thirty, the printer was having a breakdown.

Tickets spitting out in clumps. Starters, mains, modifiers, allergies, someone wanting a "chef's choice vegetarian" even though there was already a vegetarian main on the menu—but apparently this one needed a custom identity arc.

I'd been bumped up to work a little corner of the hot line. For an apprentice, that's not a promotion—it's a test. "You can either swim or we'll let you sink in front of everyone and see if you crawl out on your own."

My list was simple on paper: warm the garnish, bring the jus back to life, keep plates looking like they came from the same kitchen and not a community fundraiser.

We had a red wine jus on that week—veal stock base, reduced down, glossy, expensive, the sort of thing that makes people think the dish is "worth it" when they see the price. The base lived in a deep pan on the back, ticking over gently, and for service we'd pull off what we needed into smaller pans, reduce, mount with butter, adjust, send.

Tonight, one of those little pans was mine.

I took a ladle of base, dropped it into a sauté pan, set it over the flame. Let it reduce while I threw a garnish into another pan, listened to the pass call tickets, tried to see ten minutes into the future like everyone else on the line.

It looked right.

Thickening, darkening, catching the light.

I tossed a knob of butter in, did the swirl I'd seen everyone do since day one, and for half a second, I let myself think, *Yeah, I've got this.*

Then the dockets hit:

"Three sirloin, one lamb, all with jus, two medium rare, one blue, one well done because of course."

I fired the pans, seared the meat, dragged my garnish together. The grill was barking times, the pass was counting down, everyone was running that edge between control and collapse.

I reduced the sauce a touch more. Lifted the pan. Looked at it.

Convinced myself I didn't have time to taste it.

The base was good yesterday. It would be fine.

I told myself that and believed it because believing it was faster.

I plated.

Napkin under the plate, protein down, garnish neat, jus over the top. I sent four plates to the pass in one go and felt that tiny flicker of pride you should never, ever entertain in the middle of service.

Two minutes later, one came back.

"Table ten says the sauce is too bitter," the waiter said, doing that apologetic half-grin that means they agree but don't want to get stabbed with a fish slice.

Chef didn't look at the waiter. He looked straight at my station.

"You seasoned it?"

"Yes, Chef."

"You tasted it?"

There's no way to bluff that one.

My throat tried to form a lie and my tongue just... didn't cooperate.

"No, Chef."

He walked over, grabbed a clean spoon from the pot, dipped it into my pan and tasted. No theatrics. No commentary. Just one slow nod, like a doctor confirming what you already know.

Then he turned to the rest of the section.

"Taste this," he said.

Not the whole brigade—just the people around me. Grill, garnish, the guy on fish. Quick, efficient, one spoon each, tap-tap into the pan, taste, move on. No speeches.

Every single one of them pulled the same face.

That microscopic wince you try to hide but can't.

The jus was bitter.

Too far reduced. The wine pushed past balanced into that dry, clinging, tannic place you can't fix with a bit of sugar and charm.

Chef didn't drag it out. He tipped the contents of my pan into the bin, gave the base on the back stove a quick taste, and ladled fresh into a clean pan himself.

"You're back on garnish. I'll finish the jus," he said.

No screaming, no big scene—which somehow felt worse. It meant I wasn't important enough to yell at, just wrong enough to be removed.

Service rolled on. Plates went up. The line stayed intact.

The only thing that really took damage was my ego and a four-plate run on table ten's first impression.

It wasn't until after service—when the grills were off and the dining room had finally spat everyone back out into the night—that he circled back.

He pulled a small pot, ladled some of the same base in, set it over a low flame, and nodded at me.

"Bring this down," he said. "Slowly. Then mount it. We're not going home until it tastes right."

No big lecture. No "let me show off my wisdom" monologue.

Just work.

We stood there in the half-quiet kitchen: dishwasher still going, someone scraping the flat-top, the last of the night's adrenaline wearing off like a fading buzz. I reduced. He watched. Occasionally he'd tell me to lower the heat, or skim, or leave it alone.

When it looked right, he shoved the spoon pot towards me.

"You first," he said.

This time I tasted it before he asked.

And I actually thought about it—the texture, the balance, the way it sat. Not just "is it salty enough," but *does this say what it's supposed to say?*

It was good.

Not life-changing. Not something to write poetry about. But correct. Clean. Balanced.

He tasted it after me, gave the smallest nod, and that was it. No praise. Just acceptance.

And weirdly, that was enough.

Because the real difference wasn't the sauce.

It was the part of my brain that quietly rewired itself to never let anything leave my station untasted again.

## ChefTip #7

*"If you didn't taste it, it isn't yours."*

If your hands have touched it, your name is on it. Full stop.

Sauces, purées, dressings, oils, marinades, the last second of seasoning on a steak—if you haven't tasted it in the state it's going out, you don't know what you're serving.

Not what it was. What it is now—after reduction, after being held on the corner of the stove, after the fifteenth "just one more minute" while you juggle three other tickets.

Flavours move. Salt creeps up. Acidity dulls. Heat digs out bitterness you didn't know was hiding.

Tasting isn't insecurity.

It's responsibility.

Every spoon is you saying: *If this comes back, it comes back with my fingerprints on it—and I'm not finding out what's wrong from a waiter.*

And yeah, when you get it wrong, it stings.

Having your mistake lined up for others to taste is brutal.

But that's the part that etches itself into you so it doesn't happen again.

You taste. You adjust. You stand by it.

# TWO GLASSES ON THE PASS

Something shifted after that.

Not dramatically. Not in any way you'd notice if you weren't paying attention. Sam still rode me. Still appeared at my shoulder like some kind of culinary ghost every time my timing slipped. Still called me every name under the sun when my brunoise looked more like rubble than dice.

But there was something else now. Something underneath the bark.

---

Sam was an alcoholic.

I say that without judgement—kitchens are full of them, and he was more functional than most. He was also one of the most knowledgeable chefs I've ever worked with. Fish, specifically. The man could talk about species, sourcing, seasonality, and technique like a walking encyclopedia. Ask him about filleting a John Dory and you'd get a twenty-minute lecture you didn't ask for but would never forget.

After the standoff, he decided he was going to teach me about wine.

I didn't drink wine. Didn't drink much of anything, really—except bourbon. Aged bourbon. Neat. None of that cocktail bullshit. Wine was for people who wanted to sound interesting at dinner parties. Or so I thought.

The restaurant was the signature venue of a well-known winery in New Zealand. Which meant the bar was stocked with bottles most people only read about. And every night, after service, Sam would help himself to one.

Never asked. Never logged it. Just walked behind the bar, selected something with the kind of reverence you'd usually reserve for religious artefacts, and poured two glasses.

One for him. One for me.

At first, I was convinced this was just his excuse to polish off a bottle without drawing unwanted attention. Drinking alone looks like a problem. Drinking with a colleague looks like mentoring.

And honestly? The wine was vile.

Every sip tasted like someone had fermented a sock and called it complexity. Sam would swirl his glass, mutter something about "bramble fruit" or "tertiary notes," and I'd nod like I had any fucking idea what he was talking about. The so-called flavour profiles he spoke of were non-existent.

Until they weren't.

I don't know exactly when it happened. Somewhere along the way, things started to shift. The tannins stopped feeling like sandpaper. The acidity stopped making me wince. I'd take a sip and catch something—blackcurrant, maybe, or smoke, or something earthy I couldn't name but recognised.

Sam noticed before I did.

"There it is," he said one night, watching me over the rim of his glass. "You're actually tasting it now."

I didn't respond. But he was right.

Those nights became something I didn't expect. A ritual. The kitchen empty and cooling down, two glasses on the pass, Sam talking about wine or fish or the places he'd worked before he ended up here—not because he had to, but because this kitchen had the kind of product and standards that matched his obsession. He wasn't stuck here. Men like him never are. They just land where the product is worth their attention, and move on when something better calls.

It was a rare window where this life felt somewhat normal. Almost accepting of who I was.

Almost like I belonged somewhere.

---

I started looking for a new job a few weeks later.

Chef wasn't kidding when he said it was time to move on. He didn't want me out because I was still the useless dropkick he'd employed three years earlier. He wanted me out because he wanted me to keep the momentum. Standing still in one kitchen too long—even a good one—is how you stall. How you get comfortable. How you stop growing.

So I started looking.

Found a gig in Australia. A resort. Bigger operation, new challenges, a chance to prove that everything I'd learned in New Zealand wasn't just specific to that room.

Turns out Chef knew the resort's executive chef. Old mates from way back. He'd given me a glowing reference.

"Shining" was the word HR used when they called to offer me the position.

Shining. From the man who communicated almost exclusively in grunts and disappointment.

The closest thing *I* ever got to praise from him was three days before I left, middle of prep, when he looked at my bench and said:

"You're gonna need more of your sourdough for dinner service."

That was it.

No "well done." No "you've come a long way." Just an acknowledgement that something I made was good enough that we'd need more of it.

From him, that was everything.

---

I left three weeks later.

No fanfare. No speeches. That's not how kitchens work. You hand in your notice, you finish your shifts, you wipe down your section one last time, and you walk out the same door you walked in—except now you're someone different.

Sam didn't say goodbye. Not with words, anyway.

Last service, end of the night, I'm finishing my cleanup and he walks past my station. Doesn't stop. Doesn't look. Just drops a folded piece of paper on my bench and keeps moving.

I opened it later, out in the alley, cigarette in hand.

A reference letter. Handwritten. Two paragraphs. The closest thing to sentiment that man was capable of producing.

I still have it.

Chef shook my hand at the door. Told me not to embarrass him. Told me I was ready—then immediately undercut it by saying I'd probably fuck it up anyway, but that was fine, fucking it up was part of the job.

That was the send-off.

And then I was gone. Off to prove that everything they'd beaten into me actually meant something.

---

That was New Zealand. That was where it started.

A lot of kitchens have happened since then. Different cities. Different countries. Different ways of being told I wasn't good enough yet.

More than I can count on one hand. Each one took something and left something behind.

The scars stacked up. So did the lessons.

I think about Sam sometimes. Not often—kitchens don't leave much room for nostalgia—but sometimes.

Usually when I'm watching an apprentice make the same mistakes I made. Reaching for the wrong pan. Letting their temper show. Forgetting that the job isn't just about cooking—it's about staying in the room long enough to become someone worth keeping.

# PRODUCTION KITCHEN

That was New Zealand. That was where it started.

A lot of kitchens have happened since then. Different cities. Different countries. Different ways of being told I wasn't good enough yet.

Australia was first.

---

I landed with a suitcase, a knife kit, and absolutely no idea what I'd signed up for.

Big move for a young chef who'd only just been given CDP. New country. New kitchen. New everything.

The motto was already entrenched by then: *How hard could it be?*

Turns out, that question has layers.

I'd never worked in a hotel. Never mind a resort. The stopover on the way over was the first time I'd stayed in a hotel as a guest. Now I was supposed to work in one.

In the middle of the fucking desert.

Nothing for miles except red dirt, heat shimmer, and a kitchen I couldn't picture no matter how hard I tried.

The job title was Demi Chef of Production Kitchen.

Started at 7 a.m.

I didn't know what a production kitchen was.

But I'd said yes, packed my life into baggage allowance, and turned up anyway.

---

I arrived at 06:30.

Standard. You come early, you claim your space, you get your bearings before the room fills with bodies and the energy of service.

Except there's no one there.

Just me. And Gavin. The Executive Chef.

I'll come back to him.

---

Changed into whites. Apron tied. Boots laced.

Walked to Chef's office, knocked on the doorframe.

"Good morning, Chef. I'm looking for the production kitchen—here for my first shift."

Gavin looked up from his desk.

Smiled.

> *"Good morning, sir. Come, take a seat."*

Sir?

Take a seat?

I'm more accustomed to *muppet*, *puppy*, *bozo*. I don't think I've ever sat down in uniform before. Standing is the default. Sitting is what you do when service is over and your body gives up.

I sat.

For exactly two seconds.

It didn't feel right.

I stood again.

"Production kitchen, Chef?"

Gavin stood. Didn't say anything. Just walked me down the hall to a room I wouldn't have called a kitchen if you'd paid me.

No ovens.

No burners.

Just benches. A couple of deep sinks. Two walk-in chillers. Not even a dishie area.

*What the fuck is this.*

> *"Dane and Rob—your sous chefs—will be in at seven. Feel free to have a look around."*

Two sous chefs?

These guys must get absolutely fisted.

"Thanks, Chef."

---

I pulled up a chopping board.

Wet a cloth, slid it underneath.

Sharpened my knife.

The familiar rhythm settled me for about thirty seconds.

Then I looked around. Really looked.

And that's when I saw it.

In the sink.

Fish.

Frozen fish. Sitting in a sink full of water. Just... there. Like someone put it in and fucked off to a different postcode.

*What muppet put fish in a sink of water and left it there?*

And why in God's name was it frozen to begin with?

I'd never seen frozen fish in a professional kitchen. Ever.

And I'd definitely never seen fish left in a sink full of water like it was some kind of fucking baptism.

I drained the sink. Grabbed a holey gastro. Got the fish draining properly.

All the while on the verge of a breakdown because Chef's going to walk in, see me standing over this disaster, assume it's mine, and fire me before I've even clocked on.

*What the actual fuck.*

I was used to standards. The kind beaten into you by people who don't accept "good enough."

And this?

This was not that.

---

07:00.

The sous chefs arrived.

Scraggly uniforms. One of them wearing his glasses on top of his head like a fucking headband. They looked like they'd just woken up.

Maybe they had.

> *"G'day, mate. You must be Chris."*

"Yes, Chef."

I gestured at the sink.

"Wasn't sure where the prep list was. Found the fish though—think it might have been frozen."

> *"That's okay, mate. We'll just grab a coffee. Fish is fine—one of the boys must've left it. Just... cut some vege sticks till we get back."*

*What have I done.*

*This may possibly be the worst decision I've ever made.*

These guys are my seniors.

And it's nearly seven. Where are the others?

I bit my lip. Said nothing. Got to it.

---

Five minutes.

Four one-litre containers. One of each colour bell pepper. One of cucumber.

Perfect one-centimetre batons. Uniform. Clean.

The kind of knife work that doesn't need checking because it's already right.

I figured they'd need a dip for the day as well.

Grabbed a couple of shallots, the pepper trimmings, some pine nuts, and went looking for an oven.

Fuck me dead.

There's a whole bank of combis down the hall. Three other kitchens. The breakfast crew halfway through their shift.

I jumped into one of the free combis, roasted everything up nice and golden, headed back to production, and smashed out a quick roast pepper and sundried tomato dip.

Done.

But now what?

It's 07:00.

Where the fuck are the others?

And where is my sous?

---

07:10.

Dane and Robbie walked back through the doors.

They froze.

Looked at each other.

Looked at me.

Looked at the prep.

Back at me.

Dane picked up a baton. Held it up to the light like he'd never seen one before.

Maybe he hadn't.

When he said "vege sticks," he meant sticks. Roughly stick-shaped. Near enough.

I didn't know that language yet. Where I came from, words meant something specific. You asked for batons, you got batons. One centimetre. Uniform. Clean. Anything less and you'd be doing them again while someone explained—loudly—why your time was now their problem.

These two had been here long enough to become part of the furniture. Comfortable. Under the radar. They knew how to get the job done and go home without anyone noticing either way.

Precision wasn't the expectation.

It was a foreign object.

> *"Haha, holy shit, mate. You've only been here ten minutes."*

Forty minutes, actually.

You've been here for ten.

But I didn't say that.

Just nodded.

"What's next, Chef?"

---

That was day one.

The first of many days where I'd realise that "production kitchen" meant something completely different to what I'd been trained for.

No pass. No service. No dockets screaming at you while you juggle six pans and a ticking timer in your head.

Just prep.

Bulk prep. For functions, buffets, room service, the restaurants I'd never work in.

It wasn't bad.

It just wasn't what I knew.

And for someone who'd spent three years learning that speed and precision and pressure were the whole point, this felt like being benched.

---

Weeks passed like that.

Show up. Prep vegetables. Break down protein for events I'd never see.

Make dips, sauces, garnishes for rooms I'd never step foot in.

Clock out. Go home. Repeat.

I started to think there wasn't even a service kitchen in this place.

Then one night—coming back from an on-site function—I heard it.

That familiar sound.

A docket machine.

*New order.*

*Yes, Chef.*

I followed the sound like a junkie chasing a fix.

Down the corridor. Into the main kitchen area—usually buzzing with various teams during the day. Commissary, breakfast, staff canteen.

All gone now.

The space empty except for one little corner.

Four chefs.

Uniforms crisp. Clean.

Sections neat. Organised.

Plates under the lights.

Wait staff in three-piece suits, arms bearing waiters' cloths.

*This.*

*This is what I'd been looking for.*

---

I found a spot near the wall where I wouldn't be in the way.

Just watching.

The chefs were all different nationalities. An older Vietnamese man appeared to be running the kitchen—calling times, checking plates, moving between stations with that economy of motion you only get after decades on the line.

I'd never seen a kitchen that wasn't entirely European. Still multinational, just... European.

This was different.

A table headed off from the pass. A brief moment where the Vietnamese chef looked over to me.

"It's okay if I watch for a while, Chef?"

> *"All good, mate."*

Broken English. Thick Vietnamese accent.

But the meaning clear.

I stayed.

Watched them work through the last of the evening's service. The rhythm was familiar—the same quiet intensity I'd known in New Zealand. Chef's voice. Responses. The sound of pans and plates and controlled movement. No wasted words.

But it was unmistakably service.

Real service.

When they started wrapping up—close to 23:00—I asked if they needed a hand to clean down.

> *"All good, mate. Soon."*

Maybe he didn't understand me. *Soon?*

I stayed a little longer before heading home.

It was like a new lease on life. There was hope. A chance.

I just had to get into that kitchen.

---

Little did I know, it was already in motion.

The sous in production had already asked to have me transferred.

A day or so later, I turned up at 06:30 as I did every day. Got changed. Headed up the corridor, said hello on my way past Chef's office as I walked toward production.

Then I got stopped.

The exec sous—a Filipino woman who also ran the pastry kitchen—stepped into my path.

> *"Chef wants to see you."*

*Wants to see me.*

*Fuck.*

*This can't be good.*

I followed cautiously back to Chef's office. Knocked on the frame.

"Yes, Chef."

> *"Good morning, sir. Come in, take a seat."*

"Is everything okay, Chef?"

The door closed behind me.

---

It was nothing to worry about.

Gavin just wanted to let me know I wouldn't be in production anymore.

As of my next shift, I'd be heading to the fine dining kitchen.

That high.

That excitement.

It was back.

---

The sous chefs asked to have me transferred.

I found that out later. At the time I thought Gavin had been watching. Turns out Dane and Robbie just didn't know what to do with me.

Fair enough.

I didn't know what to do with them either.

# THE BASELINE

The fine dining kitchen changed everything.

Same resort. Same red dirt outside. Same heat shimmer making the world look like it was melting. But inside that room — four chefs, tight section, plates under heat lamps — it was a different universe entirely.

Dinner service only. Fifty covers on a big night. Tasting menus, wagyu, the kind of food that justified the price tag guests paid to fly into the middle of nowhere.

I walked in expecting a fight.

What I got was silence.

Not the hostile kind. The working kind. The kind where everyone knows what they're doing and doesn't need to announce it. Kein Do ran the pass — Senior Sous, Vietnamese, had been cooking longer than I'd been alive. He didn't yell. Didn't need to. The kitchen moved around him like water around a stone.

First service, I was given mise prep and told to stay out of the way.

Fair enough. New guy. Prove yourself.

So I did.

---

The thing about coming from a kitchen like the vineyard is that you don't know how to operate any other way.

Three years. Peeling potatoes. Peeling onions. Peeling garlic. Nothing came in a bag. Nothing came pre-cut. You wanted brunoise? You made it — from whole vegetables you'd broken down yourself, stored yourself, rotated yourself.

Stocks from scratch. Bread from scratch. Pasta from scratch. Jus reduced over hours, skimmed every fifteen minutes, strained through muslin until it was clear enough to read through.

I started at 0900 every day. Chef and Sam rolled in at ten. Finish was around 2300 — unless it was summer. Wedding season. Then it was 0200, 0300, sometimes later. Fully booked seven days a week, lunch and dinner, with weddings on Friday and Saturday nights just to keep things interesting. Winter gave us a break — no Sunday dinner, no Monday lunch. That was rest.

And through all of it: 110-pax weddings. Cooked-to-order entrées and mains. Three options each. No short-cuts. No buy-ins. Just the work.

That was my baseline.

The fine dining kitchen at the resort was different. Not lower — just different.

They made everything in-house. Proper standards. But the infrastructure around it was something I'd never seen before. The hotel had a bakery department — they made bread for every outlet. A pastry kitchen — they made desserts for everyone. Stewarding teams worked round the clock, deep-cleaning kitchens every night. All you had to do at the end of shift was clean down your section, pull the cooktops onto a trolley for the stewards, and go home.

The restaurant itself was the resort's flagship. Dinner only. Seven nights a week. Latest booking at 2000 so the kitchen could be done and out the door by 2230.

Four chefs. Sometimes five if we got lucky over peak summer. Three stewards, two on at any time.

I'd never experienced anything like it.

The work that used to bury me now barely touched the sides.

I'd finish my tasks before anyone else had started their second coffee.

And here's where it gets dangerous.

---

When the kitchen doesn't demand your standard, you have a choice.

You can coast.

No one's checking your knife work. No one's timing your mise. No one's standing over your shoulder asking why your brunoise looks like gravel. The sous chefs in production had shown me that — rolling in late, "near enough" as a philosophy, standards that existed on paper but dissolved the moment pressure arrived.

You could absolutely get away with less here.

The question is: do you want to?

---

I didn't.

Not because I was trying to impress anyone. Not because I thought someone was watching. Not because I had some grand plan to climb the ladder faster than physics should allow.

I didn't coast because I couldn't.

The vineyard had beaten something into me that I couldn't unlearn. A way of moving through a kitchen that didn't have an off switch. The standard wasn't external anymore — it wasn't about Chef's approval or Sam's silence or the fear of being called out in front of the brigade.

It was just... me.

The way I set up a board. The way I sharpened my knife. The way I stored mise and labelled containers and wiped down my section between tasks even when no one asked me to.

Those weren't habits. They were identity.

And you don't get to un-become yourself just because the kitchen stopped asking.

---

Kein Do noticed.

Not in the way senior chefs usually notice — loud, corrective, designed to make you feel two inches tall. He just... watched. Quiet. Assessing. The way you'd watch a horse before deciding whether to bet on it.

Two weeks in, he put me on entrées.

A month after that, mains.

By then, something strange was happening.

People were noticing me. Which, for an introvert who'd spent years not speaking unless spoken to, was deeply uncomfortable. I didn't mind the silence — I preferred it. Head down, work hard, don't draw attention.

But the attention came anyway.

The rest of the brigade started getting excited to work with me. Once they saw what I could do, the dynamic shifted. I'd smash out my section, then offer to help with theirs — not to show off, but to learn. Different techniques. Different approaches. And when I had something to offer back — a method that got a better result, a shortcut that didn't compromise quality — they took it. No ego. No defensiveness.

Kein Do saw all of it.

I didn't explain myself. Didn't need to.

The work spoke.

So I just kept working.

---

The promotion to CDP came four months after I arrived.

Junior Sous six months after that.

And then — barely two years after landing in Australia as a Demi Chef in a production kitchen that wasn't really a kitchen — I was offered my own venue.

CDC. Chef de Cuisine.

The bistro was technically part of the resort's dining portfolio, but it operated independently. Own building. Own brigade. Fifteen staff. Hundred and fifty covers across double sittings.

A beast.

The kind of kitchen that eats people alive if they're not ready.

---

I was twenty-four.

People ask me how.

How do you go from Demi to CDC in two years? How do you skip the queue, outpace people with more experience, more time, more everything?

And the answer isn't complicated.

It's not talent. Talent is common. Half the cooks I've worked with had more natural ability than me.

It's not connections. I didn't know anyone. I arrived in a country where my entire network was a reference letter and a handshake.

It's not luck. Luck doesn't survive scrutiny. Luck doesn't hold up under a Saturday night pre-theatre sitting when the rail is deep and the grill is screaming and everyone finds out exactly what you're made of.

It's this:

I held the standard when no one was asking me to.

Every shift. Every task. Every plate.

Not because I was being watched. Not because I was being tested. Not because I thought it would get me anywhere.

Because that's who I was now.

And you don't get to put that down just because the kitchen got easier.

## ChefTip #6

*"Your standard is yours. Protect it."*

Doesn't matter where you land.

Doesn't matter if the kitchen runs on frozen fish and pre-peeled onions. Doesn't matter if the sous chefs roll in late and no one checks your mise and "near enough" is the culture.

You do it right anyway.

Not to prove anything. Not to make anyone look bad. Not to climb faster than you should.

Because that's who you are now. And you don't get to un-become it just because the kitchen stopped asking.

Follow instruction. Respect hierarchy. Do the job you're given.

But do it to *your* standard. Not theirs.

Standards aren't external. They're not about the kitchen, or the chef, or whether anyone's watching.

They're about you.

What you'll accept from yourself when no one else is paying attention.

What you refuse to let slide even when sliding would be easier.

The Baseline you hold because it's *yours* — and crossing it would mean becoming someone you don't want to be.

That's what separates the people who stay stuck from the people who move.

Not talent. Not luck. Not knowing the right people.

Just this:

When the kitchen gets easier, your standard doesn't.

Protect it.

# COAST OR CLIMB

The promotion wasn't what I thought it would be.

Chef de Cuisine. My own venue. Twenty-four years old and running a kitchen with fifteen staff, a hundred and fifty covers across double sittings, lunch and dinner seven days a week.

On paper, it was everything I'd been working toward.

In reality, it felt like a setup.

---

I'd never set foot in the bistro before I accepted the role.

Never seen the kitchen. Never met the team. Gavin offered me the position, I said yes, and that was that.

*How hard could it be?*

I was about to find out.

---

The first time I walked through the kitchen doors, I stopped dead.

Not in awe. Not in excitement.

In horror.

The place was filthy.

Benches streaked with old grease. Floors sticky underfoot. The kind of grime that doesn't happen in a week or a month — it builds over years of nobody giving a fuck.

The walk-in chiller had containers stacked without labels, half of them growing things that definitely weren't on any menu. The freezer was icing over around the door seals — thick, white frost creeping up the walls like the whole unit was slowly being swallowed.

Spoiled food. Unlabelled containers. Cross-contamination waiting to happen.

And the worst part?

This was a semi-open kitchen.

Guests could *see* this. They could see the chaos, the stains, the complete absence of anything resembling professional standards. They just didn't know what they were looking at.

But I did.

---

The team was beaten down by years of nobody expecting anything from them.

Not incompetent — though some of them were. Just... broken. 

Their uniforms told the story before anyone opened their mouth. Whites that weren't white anymore — yellowed, stained, the kind of discolouration that no amount of bleach would fix. Aprons that looked like they'd been used to mop the floor.

This was the brigade I'd inherited.

Fifteen people who'd been coasting so long they'd forgotten there was any other way to work.

I stood in the middle of that kitchen on my first day and thought:

*What the fuck have I done.*

---

Then I looked at the menu.

And somehow, it got worse.

The napoli was canned tomatoes. Heated up. Pureed. That was it — no aromatics, no depth, no technique. Just tin-flavoured red sludge being passed off as a sauce. And this was the base for multiple dishes. *Multiple.*

The carbonara was a cream and garlic reduction with bacon that hadn't even been sautéed. Just raw, sad strips of pig fat floating in what looked like wallpaper paste.

The pastas were pre-cooked. Sitting in containers. Stuck together in congealed clumps, already overcooked before they'd even been reheated. The pesto was grey-green, stringy, bitter — the kind of thing you'd throw out if you found it in the back of your home fridge, let alone serve to paying guests.

Bought-in burger patties. Pre-made dressings. Everything that could be half-arsed had been half-arsed, and some things that shouldn't have been possible to half-arse had been half-arsed anyway.

This menu hadn't been changed in five years.

*Five years.*

And now my name was attached to it.

---

I understood then what Gavin had done.

This wasn't a reward. This wasn't recognition for two years of holding the line and climbing faster than anyone expected.

This was a test.

He knew exactly what he was handing me. The worst venue in the resort. The bottom-of-the-barrel cooks. A kitchen that had been allowed to rot because nobody cared enough to fix it.

And he wanted to see what I'd do with it.

Either I'd prove that the standards I'd become known for weren't just talk — that I could take a disaster and turn it into something worth being proud of.

Or I'd fail. Publicly. Spectacularly. And everyone would know I'd been promoted beyond my abilities.

Make or break.

Gavin was betting on make.

I wasn't going to let him lose that bet.

---

The first four months were war.

Not loud war. Not screaming matches and dramatic confrontations. Just slow, grinding, relentless pressure.

I didn't come in with speeches about transformation or new beginnings. I came in with requirements. Standards. Expectations that were now non-negotiable.

Uniforms would be clean. Whites would be white.

Stations would be set up properly. Mise en place labelled, dated, rotated.

Food would be tasted before it left the pass. Every time. No exceptions.

Walk-ins would be organised. Shelves wiped. Floors mopped. The freezer would be defrosted and maintained.

And the menu? The menu was going to change.

For the first time in five years.

---

Here's the thing about standards.

You can't demand what you won't demonstrate.

I'd learned that at the vineyard. Watched Chef and Sam hold lines they never crossed themselves. Watched them stay late, arrive early, do the work nobody asked them to do because that's what the kitchen needed. The bollockings only meant something because they came from people who'd earned the right to give them.

If I was going to ask these people to change — really change, not just comply — I had to show them I wasn't above the work I was demanding.

Good for one, good for all.

---

We didn't have the stewarding setup the main kitchen had.

No round-the-clock teams. No overnight deep-cleans. Just four boys — our own small crew, separate from the hotel's main operation.

Which meant clean-downs were on us.

Every night. Full breakdown. Benches scrubbed, floors mopped, equipment wiped, stations reset. Not a "quick tidy" — a proper clean-down, the kind where you could eat off the floor when you were done.

And once a fortnight, we went further.

The whole team rostered on. No exceptions. Floor to ceiling. Every surface, every shelf, every corner that usually got ignored. The extraction hoods came down. The cool room got stripped and scrubbed. The freezer got defrosted properly.

And I was right there with them.

Not in my whites — those had a different purpose now. Old t-shirt. Dirty jeans. Down on my hands and knees scrubbing the same grout as everyone else.

Those yellowed uniforms they'd been wearing? Now they had a place. Now they made sense. You don't wear your service whites to deep-clean a kitchen. You wear the ones that are already fucked.

The brigade noticed.

They noticed that the new CDC — the one who'd come in with all these demands and expectations and standards — wasn't standing at the pass watching them work. He was elbow-deep in the same filth they were, doing the same job, staying the same hours.

Morale doesn't come from speeches. It comes from proof.

And the proof was simple: every expectation I had for them was bolted directly to my own actions.

---

The team didn't like it at first.

I didn't expect them to.

Some of them had been there longer than I'd been cooking. They'd built comfortable little routines — show up, do the minimum, go home, repeat. Nobody had asked anything more of them in years.

Now someone was.

And they resisted.

Not openly. Not with direct challenges or confrontations. That would've been easier, honestly. Instead, it was passive — the slow drag of people who'd rather wait you out than change.

Standards slipping when they thought I wasn't looking. Shortcuts taken. Requirements ignored.

*He's new. He'll burn out. He'll realise this is how things work here and he'll adjust.*

They were wrong.

---

When someone didn't meet the standard, they got dressed down.

The same way I'd been dressed down. The same way Sam had done it — right there, in front of everyone, no privacy, no softening. The shame is part of the lesson. The audience is part of the point.

I remembered what it felt like to be on the receiving end. The heat in your face. The way your hands want to shake but you force them still. The knowledge that everyone heard, everyone saw, and now you have to either fix it or leave.

I'd stood where they were standing. I knew exactly how much it hurt.

And I did it anyway.

Because that's how standards get built. Not through memos and meetings and polite suggestions. Through public accountability. Through the understanding that fucking up doesn't disappear into a private conversation — it gets addressed in the room where it happened, in front of the people who had to work around it.

One by one, they found out I wasn't going to bend.

The guy who kept "forgetting" to label his containers? Reamed in front of the whole brigade until he understood that forgetting wasn't acceptable.

The cook who thought pre-cooked pasta was still good enough? Made to throw out the entire batch and start fresh, during service, with the whole kitchen watching.

The line cook who showed up in a stained apron because "nobody cares anyway"? Sent home to change. Docked the hour. And when he came back, I made sure he understood that I cared. That this kitchen cared now. That "nobody cares" wasn't a defence — it was the fucking problem.

I wasn't cruel about it. I wasn't theatrical.

I was just relentless.

The same relentlessness that had been applied to me.

---

Five people left in those first four to six months.

Not all at once. One by one.

The pressure would build, the expectations would stay firm, and eventually they'd decide this wasn't worth it anymore. Hand in their notice. Walk out.

I didn't get replacements.

Gavin's decision, not mine. Whether it was budget or another layer of the test, I never found out. But suddenly I had ten staff doing the work of fifteen, same covers, and now we were pushing toward more technical food than the kitchen had ever attempted.

Ten people.

Less bodies, but every single one of them more capable than the five who'd left.

---

Somewhere around the four-month mark, something shifted.

The rebellion stopped.

Not because I'd beaten it out of them — though maybe a bit of that. But because the ones who stayed started to see it.

The kitchen was cleaner. The mise was better. The food was actually *good* now — or at least, getting there. And the chaos that used to define every service? Replaced by something that felt almost like rhythm.

They'd watched me scrub floors alongside them. Watched me stay late when I could've gone home. Watched me hold myself to the same standards I was demanding from them — and they'd started to believe in themselves. In each other.

They started syncing with me.

Not fighting the current anymore. Moving with it.

I'd call a timing and hands would already be reaching for the right pan. I'd start plating and garnish would appear at my elbow without me asking. The calls and responses that used to feel forced started happening naturally — the kitchen was beginning to breathe like a single organism instead of ten separate people trapped in the same room.

---

Sonny was the first to really click.

Indian lad, late twenties, visa-sponsored. He'd come in as a Demi — solid skills, good instincts, but coasting like everyone else. Once the standards went up, he didn't just meet them. He exceeded them. Started anticipating problems before they happened. Covered sections without being asked. Took ownership in a way I hadn't seen from anyone in that kitchen.

He'd be CDP by the time I left. My strongest chef.

Then there was Jen.

Korean girl, used to work in the main kitchen commissary. She asked for a transfer down to the bistro — actually *requested* it — because she'd heard things were changing and she wanted to be part of it. The passion that girl had for food was infectious. Every new ingredient, every technique, every small evolution in the menu — she approached it like a gift. Like she couldn't believe she was being allowed to learn.

And Praveen.

Late twenties. Commis. About four foot eight and an absolute beast on larder. The man could prep faster than people twice his size, In the beginning it was rough and incosisitent, but by the end his consistency was frightening. Same cut, same portion, same presentation, every single time. You could set your watch by his mise.

These were the people who stayed.

The ones who saw what the kitchen was becoming and decided they wanted to be part of building it.

---

Nine months.

That's how long it took.

Nine months of double shifts and late nights. Lunch and dinner, seven days a week. The workload was familiar — I'd done this at the vineyard, done it harder in some ways — but the difference was that here, I was building something that was mine.

First new menu in five years.

Everything made in-house. Proper napoli — San Marzano tomatoes, fresh basil, garlic, a soffritto base that actually had depth. Carbonara done right — guanciale, egg yolk, pecorino, black pepper, no cream in fucking sight. Fresh pasta made daily. Pesto that was green, vibrant, balanced.

Handmade burger patties. Daily rotating specials. Desserts developed in-house — we still used the hotel pastry kitchen for some things, but my team was making their own specials now. Learning every day. Growing.

The bought-in garbage was gone.

The grey pesto - gone.

The congealed pasta clumps - gone.

And in their place? Food I could actually put my name to.

---

Twelve months in, the brigade was strong.

Everyone looked sharp. Uniforms crisp and white. Aprons clean. The kitchen itself was unrecognisable from the disaster I'd walked into — organised, efficient, *proud*.

That last part mattered more than anything.

You could feel it radiating from the open pass now. The energy in the dining room had changed. That electric buzz that high-end venues have — the sense that something special is happening, that the people making your food actually give a shit — it was there. It was real.

And people noticed.

---

The first sign was the executives.

Every lunch service, I'd look up from the pass and see them. Resort management. The people who ran this place. 

In our dining room.

Eating our food. 

Choosing this venue over every other option available to them.

They could've eaten anywhere. 

They ate here.

That meant something.

---

The second sign was quieter. But it hit harder.

Praveen asked if his parents could come in for dinner.

Not passing through — nobody passes through central Australia. His parents were coming from India. Specifically to see him. 

To see where he worked. 

What he'd helped build.

I said yes without hesitation.

Then I said we'd do something special.

We put together a menu just for them. A proper degustation — the kind of multi-course meal that requires the whole kitchen to move as one, every dish timed, every element perfect. The same format that had ignited something in me back at the vineyard, when I'd first felt what it meant to cook food that mattered.

Now we were doing it for Praveen's parents. During a normal service. Because he'd earned it.

I helped him cook everything. Let him call the dockets. Helped him plate. But it was *his* — his food, his moment, his parents watching from the dining room while their son ran the pass.

The hype that built in the team that night — the energy, the focus, the way everyone lifted without being asked — that's a feeling you don't forget.

It wasn't just Praveen's moment. It was proof of what we'd become.

A kitchen that would rally around one of its own. That understood some things matter more than covers and margins.

After service, his parents came to the pass.

Praveen wanted to introduce them. His mum stood back a little — quiet, reserved, the way I'd come to understand was typical of Indian families. His dad stepped forward.

> *"This is my chef."*

That's how Praveen introduced me. Not "my boss." Not "the CDC." *My chef.*

His father shook my hand. Said thank you. Wasn't much more to it than that.

But Praveen was grinning like a Cheshire cat.

And so was everyone else. The whole brigade, watching from their sections, grinning for him.

That meant everything.

---

I knew it was time to move on when the challenge stopped.

That's the thing nobody tells you about building something from nothing. There's a moment when the machine is running smoothly and the team is solid and the food is right — and suddenly it's not hard anymore.

The chaos that used to test you every single service?

The rebellion, the resistance, the constant pressure to hold the line?

All a distant memory.

What's left is maintenance. Keeping the engine running. Making sure nothing slips.

And for some people, that's enough.

For me, it was a sign.

The bistro didn't need me anymore. Sure, it needed someone who'd protect what we'd built — but it didn't need someone who'd burn themselves out chasing the next mountain.

I'd turned a sub-par venue with the bottom-of-the-barrel cooks into a kitchen that made everything in-house. Food cooked accurately, to order. The level of precision I craved, restored.

The team that remained weren't just competent — they were extensions of my own body. We moved in unison. Like puppets on strings, but in the way that means trust, not control.

I'd done what Gavin bet I would do.

Now it was time to find the next thing that would try to break me.

# GOOSE & GANDER

The first fortnightly deep-clean, I told them to dig out those yellowed uniforms.

The ones that were already stained beyond saving. The ones that finally had a purpose.

They nodded. Said yes, Chef. Went back to their stations.

But when the day came and I walked out of the kitchen toward my office, I caught the look. Quick glances between sections. The kind of silent conversation that happens when a brigade thinks they're about to get fucked.

*Here we go. He's bailing.*

*Knew it.*

*Same as the last one.*

I could feel it without turning around. The energy shift. The disappointment settling in before anything had even happened.

---

I came back in old jeans and a ratty t-shirt.

The whole kitchen went quiet.

Then someone laughed — not at me, just in surprise — and suddenly they were all talking at once. Grinning. Elbowing each other. The mood flipped so fast it was almost disorienting.

Sonny was already breaking down the six-top burner. I climbed up under the range hood, started pulling filters, passed them down to him.

"You gonna stare or you gonna scrub?"

---

That's when I understood something I'd felt but never put into words.

You can't demand what you won't demonstrate.

I was lucky. My foundations came from chefs who lived that without ever saying it. Sam scrubbing pans at 2 a.m. when the dishie was buried. Chef staying back to fix a station he could've just bollocked someone for. They never announced it. Never made it a lesson. It was just what you did.

But the kitchens I inherited? The ones I got handed because no one else wanted to fix them?

Those told a different story.

Chefs who'd barked about standards from the pass but never touched a mop. Sous chefs who'd ream you for mise that wasn't perfect while their own section looked like a crime scene. People who thought the title meant they'd graduated from the work instead of just becoming responsible for more of it.

You could feel it the moment you walked in. The resentment. The hollowness. Everyone doing the minimum because why the fuck would you give more than someone who won't give anything themselves?

That's what coasting looks like. Not in the food — in the air.

---

There's a phrase I heard somewhere along the way. Might've been my mother. Might've been a chef. Might've just been something I read and forgot the source of.

## ChefTip #5

**"What's good for the goose is good for the gander."**

Same rules. Same standard. Top to bottom.

You want your team to show up in clean whites? Show up in clean whites.

You want them to taste everything before it leaves the pass? Taste everything before it leaves the pass.

You want them to scrub grout on a Sunday? Get down on your fucking knees and scrub grout on a Sunday.

Leadership isn't standing above. It's standing with.

You can hold a title and still be part of the brigade. You can run a kitchen and still empty a fryer. You can be *Chef* and still scrub floors when that's what the kitchen needs.

The standard you set is the standard you live. No exceptions.

The moment you start exempting yourself — the moment you decide the title means you've earned the right to stop doing the hard shit — you lose the room.

Maybe not immediately. Maybe not loudly.

But you lose it.

Because your team is watching. Not for your words — for your actions. They're clocking every moment you could've pulled rank and didn't. Every time you did the shit job alongside them instead of delegating it and disappearing.

That's how trust gets built. Plate by plate. Shift by shift. One scrubbing brush at a time.

Don't forget where you came from.

Don't forget you're still one of them.

And never — not once — ask for something you're not willing to give yourself.

---

The bistro ran itself by the end.

That's the strange thing about building something properly — eventually it doesn't need you anymore. The team I'd trained could handle whatever walked through the door. The standards were theirs now, not just mine.

Which meant it was time to go.

Not up. Sideways.

Melbourne. A fine dining Italian place that had been making noise for years. They needed a CDP for pasta section.

I took the job. Took the pay cut. Dropped back down the ladder I'd just climbed.

Because running a kitchen is one thing. But I still didn't know pasta. Not really. Not the way I wanted to.

And the only way to learn something properly is to go somewhere that demands it.

# THE FRENCH CHEF IN AN ITALIAN KITCHEN

The thing about being the outsider is that nobody has to say it.

You just know.

It's in the way conversations pause half a beat when you walk into the cool room. The way jokes land and everyone laughs in sync, like they've rehearsed it except you. Not because you didn't get it, but because you weren't meant to. The way "this guy" gets muttered just loud enough for you to catch but quiet enough to deny.

I'd left a CDC position at a modern bistro hundred and fifty covers, double sittings, fifteen-plus staff under me, a beast by any definition to take a pay cut and drop a couple of rungs to CDP at a fine dining Italian place in Melbourne. On paper, it looked like career suicide. In my head, it was the only move that made sense.

You can run a kitchen for years and still not know shit.

I knew French. Classical technique, mother sauces, the whole architecture of it burned into my muscle memory. But Italian? Real Italian not the bastardised stuff that gets passed off in suburban trattorias? That was a different language. And I wanted to learn it badly enough to swallow my pride, take the demotion, and start again.

The job was pasta section. Ballsy choice, looking back. Everything made in house, pasta its own section on the menu, and while I'd made pasta before, I'd made it in French kitchens. As I was about to find out, that didn't count for much when it came to authentic ingredient pairing, technique, or execution.

But I was confident. Fast learner. Just left a joint that would've eaten most cooks alive.

How hard could it be.

Right.

What I didn't account for was how small that kitchen would feel. Four cooks in a space the size of a jail cell, and three of them had been working together for years. They moved like one organism—finishing each other's sentences, anticipating each other's calls, laughing at references I'd never understand.

I was the French chef in an Italian kitchen.

I had more drive and overall kitchen experience than even the Head Chef. But I kept that underplayed. Buried. Because the last thing I needed was to be outcast from the clique even more than I already was.

---

The pork belly thing happened about six weeks in.

I was prepping for service, my section boxed and ready to go, when Joe—the Head Chef—asked me to prep the belly for the next day's confit.

Simple enough. I'd done confit more times than I could count. Duck, pork, rabbit, goose—if it had fat and patience, I'd slow-cooked it.

I started looking for the cure mix. Salt, sugar, aromatics—standard stuff. Every kitchen I'd ever worked in had it prepped in bulk, sitting in a cambro somewhere obvious.

Couldn't find it.

I wandered the jail-cell-sized kitchen for a few minutes, checking shelves, corners, the spots where things get shoved and forgotten. Nothing.

Matt—the Sous, and the previous CDP of pasta before I'd filled the position—found me mid-search.

"You lost or just trying to find a way out?"

Laughter from the rest of the brigade. In sync. Rehearsed.

I had to retaliate. It was the only way I wasn't going to get eaten alive and have the day be another mental struggle.

"Nope. Just looking for the cure mix for the pork belly. Something I figured would be prepped in bulk, but I guess that's why you guys need help with your section and mine is boxed, ay."

His smirk flickered. The laughter stopped.

"We brine the belly," he said, less cocky now. "Recipe's in the folder."

"Too easy. Let me know if you need anything else done before service."

Deeply sarcastic. Rooted in a *fuck you, cunt* tone whilst teetering on the line of respect required by hierarchy.

I came very close to cleaning mats that night and it being my last shift.

---

After making the brine and letting it cool, I stood over the rare breed belly, fennel-infused liquid ready to go, and had a lightbulb moment.

A lesson taught early on. Widely known regardless of professional or not.

You do not mix water and fat.

Brine adds moisture. Cure removes it. And since I was confiting this thing overnight—submerging it in hot rendered fat—the physics were simple. Moisture trapped in meat plus searing fat equals explosion. Pop. Spit. The kind of violence that ends shifts early and leaves scars.

I put the question out there.

"Question—you want me to brine the belly and then confit it?"

Matt's response was immediate. Not even looking at me.

"Seriously... this guy."

I gave the warranted reason.

"Seems like a bad choice to brine something then confit it. You're adding moisture, not removing it like a cure. That fat's going to pop when it heats. Could explode."

"Just brine the fucking belly. It's how it's done here."

I held his eyes for a second longer than comfortable.

Then I brined the belly.

Not my house. Not my rules.

But I knew—somewhere in the back of my skull, with absolute certainty—that this was going to bite someone eventually.

I just didn't know it'd be Chef's eye.

---

Saturday night pre-theatre is a particular kind of violence.

First sitting hits at six. They want three courses in ninety minutes because the curtain waits for no risotto. The mad exodus starts around seven-thirty—tables practically sprinting out the door to their shows—and by then the docket machine is already becoming a demonic overlord, ready and willing to take the souls of anyone who isn't ready and on form.

The rail was loaded.

Matt was on larder and cold entrées with the apprentice—a young guy named Adam, though everyone called him Racina. Acted like an old man and was wiser than his age by decades despite being barely nineteen. I had pasta. Joe and his missus Carly were on mains and plating.

Carly was also CDP, though not really warranted of the position. She assisted in plating for both mains and occasionally pasta—when I gave her the chance, of course.

Chef called dockets as expected. The rhythm locked in. Bodies moving in that tight choreography where you stop thinking and just become the station.

"Three tasting, two à la carte on six."

"Yes, Chef."

"How long on the agnolotti?"

"Two minutes, Chef."

"Behind."

"Hot."

An order came through with one pork belly.

I watched Joe pull it from the lowboy. Watched him slide it onto a tray. Watched him reach up—up, because the salamander in that kitchen was mounted at head height—and slide it onto the bottom shelf.

I cringed.

Same as I always cringed when he did this. Knowing it was brined. Knowing it was at eye level. Knowing exactly what could happen.

But it was one of those situations. Not my house. Not my rules. Shut up and do your job.

By eight o'clock we were fully loaded on all stations. The second wave had hit. The rail was deep.

Then—simultaneously, as Chef turned around—the belly popped.

The sound cut through service noise. Sharp. Violent.

Searing fat sprayed out from under the salamander and caught Joe across the left side of his face.

Eye.

Cheek.

The soft parts that don't forgive.

Everyone froze.

That's the thing about a real emergency—the silence is louder than the chaos. One second the printer's screaming, pans are crashing, five conversations happening at once. The next, nothing. Just the hiss of the salamander and Chef on one knee, hand pressed to his face, swearing.

Scotty—the FOH manager, a hospo veteran who'd been doing this longer than most of us had been alive—pushed through the swing door.

"What do you need, Chef?"

Joe was just swearing. Holding his eye.

Scotty didn't wait for an answer. He grabbed his keys, handed them to a food runner.

"I'm parked in the Chinatown laneway. Go get my car and take Chef to A&E."

And that's exactly what happened.

But now who was the captain of our about-to-sink ship?

Well. That would be Matt. The Sous Chef.

Except Matt had never run a Saturday night service. He'd only done early weekday nights—cruisy twenty-five to thirty covers, nicely spread. Not sixty to eighty covers for three courses in three hours after being fisted by a pre-theatre sitting.

I looked at him.

He was pale. Actually pale. The colour you go when your brain is running calculations it doesn't like the answers to.

I found joy in the moment.

Just a flash of it—petty, unprofessional, deeply human. Six weeks of "this guy" and cure mix lectures and in-sync laughter, and here he was. Frozen.

"Looks like you're up, Chef."

All the while, tables are still being called away. Every second that passes is an eternity in a kitchen.

"But... but... I—"

The only barely audible words that came from the Sous's mouth.

"For fuck's sake, I'll do it then."

I laughed. Actually laughed.

Scotty had reappeared—must've handed Joe off to Dylan and come straight back. He caught my eye across the line.

"You got this, mate?"

"Does the Pope touch little boys?"

"Good man."

He turned away to work his magic and buy me a couple of minutes in the dining room.

---

I grabbed the next docket as I assessed the state of everything in motion.

Then I proceeded to do what I did best. The thing I loved most about being a chef.

Lead service.

And just to boot, I continued to cook and plate my own section at the same time.

"Two agnolotti, one pappardelle, mains to follow in four."

"Yes, Chef."

"Matt, how long on the burrata?"

"Thirty seconds."

"Make it twenty. Table eight's been waiting."

"Yes, Chef."

The rhythm came back. Different now—tighter, more deliberate, running through me instead of around me. I could feel the kitchen settling, finding its feet, trusting that someone was steering.

Carly's hands stopped shaking around the forty-minute mark. Matt found his timing. Even Raj—the silent Nepalese dishie I'd never heard speak—started anticipating my calls, running pans back before I had to ask.

The night went without a hitch.

Not a single complaint. Not one remake. Not one diner aware of the close-to-complete nuclear-grade meltdown that almost occurred in the kitchen.

Just pure orchestrated success.

---

The last ticket went out and Scotty came into the kitchen—which was oddly silent—with a round of Peronis. The staple knock-offs in this particular kitchen.

He handed one to Matt. One to Carly. Then pressed an extra into my hands.

"Well deserved, Chef. Thank you."

"Here to help. That's what I do."

I said it with a cheeky smirk. Couldn't help myself.

We filed out into the back laneway for some fresh air and quick respite.

There were two other fine dining restaurants in the block that shared the same internal laneway—another Italian and a street-hawker-style joint. On a normal night, there'd be banter between crews, competitive shit-talk, the kind of solidarity that only exists at midnight between people who've just survived the same war.

Tonight, it was quiet.

I never spoke much anyway—not exactly the social type outside the kitchen. But tonight the others just sat there in silence too.

I broke it.

Poorly placed. A shiv of *I told you so*.

"I've got a good recipe for cure mix if you want to change from brine."

Crickets.

"Too soon then, I guess."

The food runner got back a few minutes later with Joe in tow. Chef was bandaged up, one eye covered. Looked like he'd done three rounds with a deep fryer. The doctors wanted him back in two days to check for damage.

Racina—still out in the laneway with us, probably processing everything he'd just witnessed—turned to me.

"When did you learn to run service, Chef?"

I took a long pull on my beer.

"Been running kitchens for years, bruva. Could do it with my eyes closed."

Silence again. Different this time.

Matt looked up.

"You never mentioned that before."

"You never asked."

I let that land.

"In fact, I'm barely part of the team most days."

It was acknowledged. Not in words—just a quiet exchange of looks in my peripheral as I sank my second beer.

The moment stretched.

Then I pushed off the wall.

"Come on. Let's go—we've got a kitchen to clean."

Everyone jumped up.

"Yes, Chef."

I bumped shoulders with Matt as we walked in.

"You wanna take over yet, or you happy with me to finish up?"

"Go for it. It's all yours."

---

Chef was off for just over three weeks.

Matt and I shared the kitchen leadership. I ran the weekends—the ones that could go sideways fast, the ones where you needed someone who'd seen worse and kept moving. During the week, I helped him find the path he wasn't being given under Joe. The development he should've had years ago.

I still wasn't part of the family.

But I was definitely part of the team.

And there was a lot more respect.

# I CAN COOK BETTER THAN YOU

I've been doing this long enough to know what I'm worth.

Long enough that my hands don't feel heat the way yours do. Long enough that my
body keeps score in ways I stopped noticing years ago. Long enough that I've
stopped counting the kitchens, the covers, the chefs who broke me down and the ones
I broke down in return.

So when someone tells me they can cook, I listen.

And then I wait for the punchline.

Because it's always coming. It's always some version of: "I made a risotto once and
it turned out great." Or: "My partner does this amazing Thai curry." Or my personal
favourite: "My Mum's the best cook I know."

I get it. You think you can do what I do.

But here's the thing—you can't.

There are countless differences between my cooking environment and yours. My
kitchen stove top does not have a "Save As" button, okay? If I fuck up in the
kitchen, that's it. There's no undo, there's no: "Oh well, the guests will
understand."

Guests will NEVER understand. Nor do they appreciate that I can cook a steak to
medium rare, every time. Because when my guest is paying fifty-eight dollars for
that steak, do you really think they're going to be satisfied when it's not right?
A simple: "Oh that's okay love, medium rare, give or take a few degrees." They may
as well be saying: "Here, while you're at it take my wallet and the keys to my
Mercedes E350... And I'm an understanding, laid-back guy—of course you may fuck my
wife."

You just sit in your cushy little household kitchen with your nine-hundred-dollar
KitchenAid that you have only mustered how to whisk in, and casually stress over
trying to pull the garlic bread up at the same time as Donna Hay's Pasta a la
Primavera without everything going stodgy and cold for your five other guests who
will probably still accept you at least as a casual acquaintance even if it is only
for a Christmas party invite—sadly, my reviewers are not the same.

I'll have seven pans going, three of them bipolar and temperamental sauces, two
fish in the oven, a salad to prep, all while watching the kitchen hand with one eye
who keeps wiping the plates clean with a tea towel without actually washing
them—whilst not forgetting that table seven is half gluten-free even though it's
not on the docket, and tables thirty-four, seventeen, and three are coming up on
mains all at once because front of house are cunts.

No worries, mate.

But it's even different for us chefs at home. I guarantee you, when I make a meal
it's all: "Thanks handsome man, please pass the potatoes." But you bet your arse if
my girl is cooking for me, I better act like she's single-handedly built the Great
Wall of China, blindfolded, with nothing more than a toothpick and some intuition.

---

But as far as your Mum being the best chef you know—shush, just stop it. Your Mum
might be the best cook in the world, for sure. Hell, my mother makes the best bbq
chicken I have ever tasted. And my Dad? Well... he wasn't much of a cook, but his
curried sausages. I can guarantee you, it can't be beaten. So I do believe you when
you say your half-Lebanese mother makes the best... I don't know, falafel.
Whatever—my point is, they make it for you. They make it for your brothers and
sisters and family friends.

They don't make it every single night, for fifty-five or one hundred and five
covers, plated perfectly, cooked perfectly, over and over, repeatedly and
consistently—and fast.

My Dad can't do that. Do you know WHY he can't do that?

Because he's a fucking Cabinet Maker, not a chef.

*"No one is born a great cook, one learns by doing." — Julia Child*

I like that quote. I like to think that the techniques I have repeated day in and
out for however many years mean something. I like to think that what we do is kind
of like porn. You can watch it, be fascinated by it, it will entice your intrigue
and tickle your curiosity—but try it at home, it won't look or sound nearly the
same or as satisfying, and the majority of you are sensible enough to realise that
it's probably not wise to try and act it out.

You can like cooking all you want. It doesn't mean it will like you back.

And it's the little things that make us different. For example:

- I can chop without looking.
- I don't follow a recipe. I laugh at recipes. I look at them, study them, then
  laugh at them and think: "Well fuck, that's not gonna happen—I'm doing it this
  way."
- I can pick up a piece of confit duck with my bare hands. It doesn't bother me one
  bit; I lost all feeling in them years ago anyway. The sensation in my index
  finger is left with the skin that is now attached to the grill of my first job.
  There it shall remain.
- We don't have Band-Aids; we have masking tape.
- Where my palms once were, I now have scars and calluses.
- I don't have an eight o'clock reservation for two at a delightful little wine bar
  with my significant other—I have a second sitting, a headache, and I'm running
  out of leeks.

Do you see me walking up to a neurosurgeon suggesting that: "Hey, I played
Operation as a kid. I can do this shit too."

No. I'm not that fucking stupid.

So please. Stop trying to patronise me and my fellow industry comrades by telling
me you can do what I do. You can't. And when you try, you just look like an idiot.

When you try—you do things like go on MasterChef.

And mate, I've got the popcorn ready.

---

But here's the thing about confidence like this—it doesn't come free.

You don't walk into your first kitchen with opinions. You walk in with nothing.
Less than nothing. You walk in as the problem everyone has to work around until you
figure out how to stop being one.

And if you're lucky—really lucky—you find someone brutal enough to turn you into
something useful.

But it's not just the brutal ones who shape you.

It's the ones standing next to you when everything's on fire.

# THE PEOPLE WHO LAUGH WHILE YOU'RE DROWNING

There's a very specific kind of love that exists in real kitchens.

Not brunch joints with latte art and matching aprons.

I mean proper rooms. Serious ones. The kind where the food almost never goes
wrong—and if it does, everybody remembers the date and time like a funeral.

From the outside, it probably looks like bullying.

From the inside, it's the only reason any of us still have a pulse.

It always hits me on those nights when the rail is full and nobody's breathing.

You know the ones.

Walk-in already stripped. Prep list still half-checked. Private function in the
back. Full book in the front because management likes saying "yes" to every booking
and "no" to reality.

By six o'clock the docket rail is doing that slow, dangerous lean. Grill is
snarling at the printer. Pastry's already tight because desserts changed yesterday
and nobody told her until the mise was done.

I'm in my corner trying to keep my station immaculate and my head clear, working at
that speed where every movement is deliberate and nothing can be sloppy, because in
rooms like this "near enough" means "do it again".

It's when you're running that close to the line that you feel it—that awful
tightness in your chest that whispers:

*If you slip, you'll take everyone with you.*

Then it starts.

The chorus.

**"BEHIND".**

**"HOT".**

**"YES CHEF".**

**"IN THE OPEN YOUR FUCKEN EYES SECTION".**

To anyone else, it sounds like aggression.

To us, it sounds like breathing.

---

There was one Saturday I still feel in my bones.

Fully booked from six till late. Tasting menu on. Couple of VIPs the owner wouldn't
shut up about.

My station was veg and garnish—which, in a good kitchen, is not "just veg". It's
pace and precision and timing and discipline. Every plate walks past you. You're
the hinge on every main.

Calls start stacking:

> *"away on tasting mains, table 15 mains, all together."*
>
> *"how long entrees table seven."*
>
> *"three minutes on scallops table twenty"*
> *"yes chef"*

We're in the groove—not loose, just tight in the right way. Setup's sharp, plates
are landing clean, everything hitting that "of course it's perfect" level you live
for.

Then one docket drops with a mod that changes everything on my side: no alliums, no
butter, separate pan for one component. Totally doable, but it means a different
pathway in my head.

I hear it, I repeat it, I mark it... and somewhere between "yes, Chef" and my hands
moving, my brain tries to process two dockets at once.

For half a heartbeat, I reach for the wrong pan.

I don't sauce it. I don't plate it.

I catch myself a fraction of a second before anything irreversible happens and
shift to the clean pan. The guest will never know how close that got.

But inside my head?

That half-second wobble feels like I've crashed the entire service.

Chef clocks it immediately.

Of course he does.

> *"Garnish how long. mains—two beef, one duck, one veg. You've got purée, beans,
> potatoes, and the mod on ten. Call your times."*

"Four on beef and duck, five on veg, six on the coeliac."

> *"three minute on mains—two beef, one duck."*

My world shrinks around me.

Grill adjusts his own timing by a minute without saying anything, just calls:

> *"Beef is plus one, Chef."*

Like it was his decision. Buying me time.

Fish slides an extra pan onto my free burner with the component I nearly
forgot—no lecture, just a tap on the bench.

Pastry, miles from the line, somehow feels the shift in volume and sends a ramekin
of something set and sugary to my station. She drops a spoon next to it and
mutters:

> *"Eat or I'll murder you."*

Which in kitchen language translates to: Your blood sugar is tanking and I'm not
carrying your dead body out of here.

Nothing about it looks soft.

Nobody says, "Are you okay?"

Chef still calls me a muppet when I reach for the salt instead of the tongs.

But the whole kitchen tightens around me just enough that there is zero chance of
an actual fuck-up hitting a plate.

The guests get what they always get:

Exact. Precision. Perfection.

No hint that, for about thirty seconds, one of the cogs in the machine felt like it
was about to shake itself loose.

---

After service, we're out in the alley. Backs against the wall. Staffies sweating in
our hands. Everyone running on fumes and adrenaline.

Everything hurts.

Feet, shoulders, fingers, the bit of your spine you only discover exists after
twelve hours on the pass.

Someone brings up a ticket we turned around in a way that should've been
impossible. Someone else reminds Chef of the time he called a re-fire on a whole
table because he decided, unilaterally, that the plates had sat in the pass twenty
seconds too long.

Then, because we're arseholes, they circle back to me.

> *"Remember when apprentice almost baptised the allergy plate in jus tonight?"*

Grill says it with the grin already loaded.

They all laugh—its not to be cruel.

It's recognition.

They've all had that half-step slip, that almost-mistake nobody outside the room
ever knew about.

Grill nudges my boot with his.

> *"Didn't happen though, did it? That's the point."*

Chef doesn't join in the pile-on. He just looks over, takes a drag of his
cigarette:

> *"alright lets go."*

People hear how we speak to each other and think we hate each other.

They hear the swearing, the sarcasm, the way "cunt" gets used as both insult and
endearment, and they assume it's toxic.

They don't see the other half.

The senior chef who quietly re-does your mise after service, not because it was
unusable, but because he wants tomorrow to start clean for you.

The grill cook who takes the bollocking that should've been yours because you were
the one who misheard the call, and he decides it stops with him.

The dishie you'd sell your soul for because they keep your world moving, and if
anyone from front of house talks down to them, suddenly the whole brigade is
standing there, very polite and very, very dangerous.

We will call each other every name under the sun from six till ten.

But if someone outside the line takes a shot at one of ours?

We remember we're a pack.

You might be "an idiot" at 7:05 when you forget a garnish, but at 7:06, if a waiter
says something sideways, you're:

*"My idiot, actually."*

---

That's the thing about this life.

You don't earn your place once. You earn it every service. Every shift. Every time
you show up and prove you're still worth keeping.

# YOU'RE ONLY AS GOOD AS YOUR LAST SERVICE

This first one isn't about cooking.

It's about time.

---

There's this thing that happens when you're young and invincible.

You think there's always more.

More hours. More days. More chances to say the thing you should've said, do the
thing you kept putting off, be present in the moment you let slip past because you
were tired, or busy, or just not paying attention.

You tell yourself: tomorrow.

Tomorrow I'll call. Tomorrow I'll visit. Tomorrow I'll sit down and actually be
there instead of half-listening while my brain runs through tomorrow's prep list or
next week's problems or whatever bullshit feels urgent in the moment.

Tomorrow. Tomorrow. Tomorrow.

And then one day, there is no tomorrow.

Not for that person. Not for that conversation. Not for that version of your life
where you still had the chance to choose differently.

---

I'm not going to tell you some long story here.

I'm not going to dress it up in kitchen metaphors or make it clever.

Some lessons don't come with a funny anecdote and a punchline. They just
arrive—unannounced, uninvited—and rearrange everything you thought you knew about
what matters.

This is one of those.

---

## ChefTip #1

**Make time for the people who matter. Not tomorrow. Now.**

Not when the service is over. Not when the season slows down. Not when you've hit
that next milestone or ticked that next box or finally feel like you've earned the
right to breathe.

Now.

Because life doesn't wait for you to be ready.

I've learned a lot of things over the years.

How to break down a fish. How to balance a sauce. How to survive a Saturday night
that should've killed us all.

But the most important thing I've ever learned can't be taught in a kitchen.

It's this:

**Tomorrow is not guaranteed.**

The most important role you will ever have—the most important position—is you.

Make time for your family. Your friends.

Give them your best service.

Make the moment matter.

Here's the thing about family, though.

Sometimes it's not the one you're born into.

Sometimes it's the people standing next to you when the rail is full and the
printer won't stop screaming and your feet stopped existing two hours ago. The ones
who call you a cunt at 7:15 and cover your section at 7:16 without being asked.

Sometimes family is forged in heat.

Literally.
