# THE ONION WILL NOT WAIT FOR YOU

There’s a very specific moment in every young cook’s life when the kitchen stops being whatever you’d built up in your head and starts becoming what it actually is: a pit of chaos, heat, and self-inflicted hurt.

You learn that quickly.

There was a lot of peeling in those early days. Potatoes, onions, garlic. Nothing arrived conveniently prepared, and whatever happened to it next started with someone standing at a bench doing the part nobody put on the menu.

Quite often, that someone was me.

I wanted to cook. There was cooking happening all around me, being done by people whose hands seemed to know where they were going before mine had worked out where to start. Meanwhile, I had an onion, a knife, and a fairly limited understanding of how much there was to get wrong between the two.

I was trying to dice onions like a human metronome—consistent, clean, tidy. Instead, some of the pieces looked like they’d been taken hostage and tortured for information.

Not disastrously wrong. Nobody needed to evacuate. But put them beside each other and the difference was obvious, regardless of how long I’d spent trying to get them right. For me, that moment arrived the day I realised an onion doesn’t give a single shit about my personal wellbeing.

Everything had to cook evenly. It all had to belong in the same dish. Whatever size the cut called for, that was the size you were supposed to produce, right through the pile. You couldn’t put the good ones on top and hope nobody investigated further.

The people around me could do that without giving it their full attention. They could keep working while listening to the pass, answering a question, keeping track of something else. I was still giving the onion considerably more of my day than it deserved.

It was tempting to watch their speed and think that was the bit I needed to copy. But moving a knife faster wasn’t going to make uneven cuts more consistent. It was just going to leave me with a larger quantity of shit work.

Chef showed me once—once—and somehow made the demonstration feel like a question about what the fuck I was doing in his kitchen.

Square it up before you started cutting. Give it a flat surface to sit on. Obvious, once someone had shown you. Unfortunately, standing there with a knife in my hand, I was discovering that *once someone had shown you* was a fairly significant qualification.

He showed me as though he was pointing out something I’d inexplicably chosen to ignore. There I was, wanting to cook, watching what everyone else was doing, and he was having to explain how to stop a fucking vegetable rolling around on a chopping board.

The knife in my hand wasn’t doing much for my case.

It was such a small thing to be shown. That was what made it worse. And now that he’d shown me, there was the expectation that he wouldn’t have to show me again.

So I kept doing it. Whole vegetables, broken down by hand, the same cuts repeated until they started coming out the way they were supposed to. Then repeated again, because getting one right didn’t finish the prep.

---

I started at nine. Chef and Sam came in at ten. An hour at the bench before the two people most capable of finding something wrong with what I was doing arrived to have a look.

There was plenty to occupy it.

At the vineyard, we made everything from scratch. Stocks, bread, pasta, sauces. The vegetables had to be broken down, stored properly, rotated. The jobs I wanted to get near depended on the jobs I was already doing, although that was easier to appreciate when I wasn’t halfway through another pile of fucking onions.

I could watch something leave the pass and want to be the person who’d cooked it. It took longer to appreciate how much of that plate had been decided before service, while there were still hours to go and nobody in the dining room.

The cut. The portion. Whether something was ready to use or still needed another pair of hands to finish it.

That was where my work went. Into someone else’s next job. An uneven cut didn’t become their problem any less because I’d tried hard, and a container full of something almost finished wasn’t much help to someone who needed it now.

The hours gave me plenty of opportunity to get acquainted with that arrangement. Nine in the morning until eleven at night was familiar. Summer pushed the finish out to two or three in the morning, sometimes later. Lunch and dinner, with weddings on top.

A hundred and ten wedding guests, choosing between three entrées and three mains, all cooked to order. That was the sort of work the kitchen could put out. I was still learning to make sure my contribution didn’t need fixing before anyone could use it.

Watching Chef and Sam work, the attraction was obvious. They could do the things I wanted to do. What was less exciting was how much of getting there appeared to involve doing the same ordinary things properly, over and over, without deciding I was above them.

There was no shortage of repetition. The prep got used. The containers emptied. Then there was another service to get ready for.

Getting through the pile bought you the next job.

---

Once you’ve chopped ten thousand onions, you stop caring about tears. Somewhere in all that repetition, the job that once took your full attention becomes something your hands can get on with while you listen to what else the kitchen needs.

That was the speed I’d been watching. There was a considerable amount of peeling between seeing it and being able to do it.

I hadn’t chosen kitchens……well I hadn’t actually chosen kitchens at all up until this point and it certainly wasn’t because I  wanted to spend particularly that much time with an onion. But the food I wanted to cook still needed someone who could be trusted to complete the fucking prep properly first.

Chef’s demonstration stayed with me long after I stopped needing to think about it. He hadn’t presented it as advice I could take or leave. He’d shown me how the job was done, and from then on, that was how I was expected to do it.

So, for anyone standing there with a knife in their hand, waiting for the more interesting part: this is part of it.

## ChefTip #10

*“Square your damn ingredients before you chop. Always.”*

Give yourself a stable surface to work from. If the thing is rolling around underneath your knife, sort that out before trying to cut it faster.

You want consistent pieces. The size the job calls for, right through the batch. Something the next person can pick up and use without having to finish your work for you.

The speed comes with doing it properly often enough. Trying to skip that part just means getting to the end sooner and discovering you’re still not finished.

It’s a small correction. Easy to show someone. Easy to dismiss when you’re impatient to get on with something that feels more like cooking.

I was that impatient.

Chef still had to stop and show me.

You’ve just had the explanation without having to stand in front of him holding the evidence. Make use of it.
