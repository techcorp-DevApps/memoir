# NEVER TAKE YOUR EYES OFF THE STOCKPOT

There's this moment somewhere between your first real burn and the day you finally stop flinching when someone shouts "Behind!"—when you realise the kitchen isn't simply a workplace. It's a living thing. Loud. Unpredictable. Slightly unhinged. And it has absolutely no interest in accommodating your feelings.

I'd just graduated from "onion hostage negotiator" to someone Chef trusted enough to give a job with actual consequences. Which, as it turns out, was its own kind of trap! because the second a chef believes you're competent, that's when they start handing you tasks fully capable of ruining your entire week.

He gestures toward this monstrous stockpot simmering on the back burner.

The thing looked like it contained the remains of a medieval feast. Bones, herbs, scraps, steam swirling up like it was exhaling old secrets.

"Keep an eye on that," he says.

I nod, pretending I'm ready. My eyes flick to the pot and away too fast. My hand finds the edge of the bench before I've decided to reach for it. But that's the thing about kitchens: they don't wait for you to feel ready. They assume readiness is something you'll discover in the aftermath.

For the first few minutes, I'm hyper-attentive.

Watching the surface ripple. Listening to the gentle murmur of boiling stock. My shoulders come down an inch for the first time all shift.

Then service starts tightening around us like a vise.

The grill cook is losing his temper about ticket times. The pastry chef is whispering threats to a tray of soufflés. Someone down the line has dropped a full container of béchamel and is contemplating their life choices.

And in the middle of this swirling madness, I turn—just for a heartbeat—because someone yelled my name with the exact tone people usually reserve for news of a death or a pregnancy.

When I face the pot again, it's too late.

The damn thing is overflowing.

Not a gentle spill—a full-blown uprising. Foam marching down the sides. A slow, golden flood taking the express route across the stovetop and onto the floor.

All I can think is:

*So this is it. This is how careers end—drowning in poultry-flavoured regret.*

Chef appears behind me, silent at first. That's always worse.

Then, in that low voice chefs use right before they decide whether to yell or laugh:

"You turned your back on it, didn't you?"

And here's the most painful part:

He wasn't angry. He was disappointed.

Which, in kitchen terms, is borderline devastating.

## ChefTip #9

*"Never turn your back on a stockpot. If you're responsible for it, you're present for it."*

A pot doesn't wait for your attention.

It doesn't care about incoming dockets or your rising panic or the grill cook's latest meltdown.

It demands presence—real presence—the kind you can't fake.

You skim it. You check the heat hasn't crept up. And if you have to leave your station, you hand it off—out loud, to someone who acknowledges it. Anything less and you're just hoping.

Stock is hours of work and the foundation of every sauce, jus, and braise that comes out of that kitchen. Boil it over and you haven't just made a mess—you've told Chef his time means less to you than whatever pulled your eyes away.

Stay with it. Stay connected. Stay aware.
